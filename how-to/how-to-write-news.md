# News Rulebook

Rules for writing news copy that editors run and readers finish. Read this document to learn the craft; give it to a model to apply it. It governs any draft written for a newsroom or a wire: a hard-news story, a feature, an analysis, a briefing, or a press release. The document under revision is called the draft. These rules are audit criteria, applied one at a time; they are not simultaneous generation constraints.

## 1. Protocol

Execute these steps in order when given a draft:

1. Read the whole draft and name its genre - hard news, feature, analysis, briefing, or press release - before changing anything.
2. Audit the draft against sections 2 through 10. Apply section 10 only to press releases and wire copy; skip it otherwise.
3. Rewrite each violation in place. Preserve reported facts, quotations, and attribution; change how the copy reads, not what it reports.
4. Verify every name, title, number, date, and quotation against a source. Where a fact resists verification, flag it and leave it unchanged rather than smoothing it into certainty.
5. Run the section 11 checklist on the revision and fix what fails.

## 2. Structure

- Order information by descending importance so an editor can cut from the bottom without losing essentials, the inverted pyramid. Apply this to hard news and press releases.
- Answer who, what, when, where, why, and how by the end of the opening; carry any unanswered question into the next paragraph.
- Place the nut graf, the sentence that states why the story matters, by the second paragraph and no later than the fifth. In breaking news, let the lead do that work.
- Keep paragraphs to one or two sentences, and give each direct quotation its own paragraph.
- Match structure to genre: inverted pyramid for hard news; an anecdote or scene plus a nut graf for features; a so-what synthesis for analysis; stacked item-plus-blurb for briefings.
- Close a feature on a kicker, a final image, quote, or callback to the lead, because inverted-pyramid habits waste feature endings; don't let the piece trail off.
- Don't bury the lead beneath setup or chronology; state the news first, then supply the context.

## 3. Leads

- Write the lead as one sentence of 25 to 35 words with at most one comma; a summary lead runs shorter, 15 to 25 words.
- Put the main verb within the first seven words and keep it active.
- Match the lead type to the story: a 5W summary for hard news; delayed identification when the name means nothing to readers yet; an anecdote or scene for features; a synthesis for analysis.
- Make the lead specific; a broad lead informs no one and entices no one.
- Don't crowd the lead with unfamiliar names, adjectives, or adverbs; move secondary names to a later paragraph.
- Place attribution at the end of the lead when the source must appear there.
- Draft a plain 5W sentence first, then sharpen it; completeness precedes flourish.

## 4. Headlines

- Make the headline accurate to the story; don't oversell it and cheat the reader, and don't undersell it and hide the news. Accuracy has no "close enough."
- Open the headline with its subject and an active verb, and put the strongest words first, because the opening is the best-attended position.
- Match the headline's tone to the story; don't pun on a grave subject.
- Make the headline stand on its own away from the article, because readers meet it on social feeds without context.
- Follow the outlet's house style, and confirm the headline states the story's actual point, not yesterday's news or a side issue.

## 5. Sourcing, attribution, and verification

- Verify before publishing; neither speed nor format excuses inaccuracy.
- Gather at least three credible first-hand sources, at least two of them human, before filing a non-breaking story; hold to any stricter newsroom rule.
- Attribute every fact and every opinion to its source, named with an identifying title on first reference and by surname after.
- Prefer named sources; grant anonymity only when the source faces real harm and the information is available nowhere else, and state why it was granted.
- Don't write "sources" when only one source exists.
- Treat quotations as inviolable: paraphrase inelegant speech rather than clean it up or expose it to ridicule, and alter a quote only to drop a redundant word without changing its sense.
- Seek and include a response from anyone the story criticizes or accuses.
- Before publishing, ask how the copy would withstand a challenge or a denial; try to disprove your own story, not only to confirm it.
- Correct errors promptly and openly.

## 6. Voice and language

- Write in the active voice by default; use the passive only when the receiver matters more than the actor, the actor is unknown, or the focus belongs on the victim.
- Prefer short, plain, familiar words, and translate jargon into them.
- Default the attribution verb to "said." Don't pair "claimed" with a statement of fact, because it signals disbelief; reserve "refute" and "rebut" for something actually disproven, and use "say" otherwise.
- Cut hype nouns like "flood" and "surge" when the numbers are modest; state the number instead.
- Show with concrete, sensory detail and let sources carry judgment; don't stamp people with subjective adjectives such as "brilliant" or "controversial" in your own voice.
- Quote for color, emotion, and opinion; paraphrase dry facts a quotation would only slow.
- Use one term per thing throughout a piece; a synonym reads as a new referent.

## 7. Style mechanics

Reason: US desks and the wires that feed them share the AP Stylebook, so AP-conformant copy needs the least editing to run.

- Spell out whole numbers below 10 and use figures for 10 and up; use figures regardless for ages, percentages, money, dimensions, times, and dates.
- Spell out any number that opens a sentence, except a year.
- Capitalize a formal title only directly before a name; lowercase it after the name or standing alone.
- Omit the serial comma in a simple series, and add it only where the sentence is ambiguous without it.
- Put commas and periods inside closing quotation marks.
- Set the dateline as CITY, then state or country, matching the place the reporting was done; don't dateline desk-assembled copy as though it were reported on location.
- Mention race, gender, sexuality, age, or disability only when relevant to the story, and follow current stylebook terms for each.
- Pick one desk's style and hold it throughout; where desks diverge - courtesy titles on second reference, capitalization of Black and White, month-day-year versus day-month-year - don't mix conventions.

## 8. Objectivity and opinion

- Keep your own opinion out of news copy; report what sources say and let attributed voices carry views.
- Label analysis, commentary, and opinion as such, so a reader arriving without section context can tell them from news.
- In analysis, ground every judgment in reported facts and named sources, and don't drift into advocacy.
- Practice due impartiality: weight positions by evidence rather than splitting the difference, and don't manufacture false balance between a supported claim and an unsupported one.
- Treat objectivity as a method, the discipline of verification, not as a neutral tone laid over unchecked assertion.

## 9. Genre fit

Name the genre before writing, because it sets structure, lead, voice, and ending.

- Hard news: report timely facts; inverted pyramid; summary lead; neutral voice; ending optional.
- Feature: explore experience or trend; narrative or thematic structure; anecdote or scene lead plus nut graf; reported but distinct voice; end on a kicker.
- Analysis: explain why an event matters; explanatory path; synthesis lead; expert contextualizing voice; end on the takeaway. Label it as analysis.
- Briefing: package several stories for scanning; stacked items; each item a headline plus a one-to-three-sentence blurb with why it matters; utility voice; link out.

## 10. Press releases and wire copy

Apply this section only to press releases and wire copy.

- Structure the release as headline, dateline and lead, body with quotes, boilerplate, then contact; mark the end with a centered ### where house style expects it.
- Write in the third person and inform rather than sell; a release that reads as an advertisement gets discarded.
- Hold the release to 300 to 500 words, the boilerplate to 100 words or fewer, and body links to one to three, about one per hundred words.
- Keep the headline under 100 characters, 51 to 100 performs best, and put no hyperlink in it, because search engines may not read a linked string as the headline.
- Front-load keywords into the headline, subhead, and first 100 words, and write them in natural language rather than stuffing.
- Cut PR cliches; replace "thrilled to announce," "industry-leading," and "game-changing" with a specific fact or number, because an unprovable superlative is what makes an editor delete the release.
- Write each quote as words a person would actually say, and don't let a quote merely restate the lead.
- For a public company, place the ticker on first mention, tie any forward-looking-statement safe-harbor to company-specific risks rather than boilerplate, and mark a trademark once at its first or most prominent use.
- Distinguish the three forms: a press release announces publishable news; a media alert invites coverage of an upcoming event in 150 to 300 logistics-first words, three to five days ahead; a news article is independent journalism, not either of those.

## 11. Checklist

Run these checks on the finished draft. Each answers yes or no; each no returns to its section.

- The most important fact stands first, and the nut graf appears by the fifth paragraph. (2)
- The lead is one sentence, 35 words or fewer, active, with its verb early. (3)
- The headline is accurate, self-contained, and states the real point. (4)
- Every fact and opinion is attributed, and non-breaking copy rests on at least three sources, two of them human. (5)
- Every name, number, date, and quote is verified, and anyone accused was asked to respond. (5)
- The copy is active-voice by default, "said" is the default attribution verb, and no "claimed" sits on a fact. (6)
- Numbers, titles, datelines, and the serial comma follow one desk's style throughout. (7)
- No unlabeled opinion sits in news copy, and analysis is marked as analysis. (8)
- The genre is named, and structure, lead, voice, and ending match it. (9)
- Every release runs 300 to 500 words with a headline under 100 characters and no cliches. (10)
