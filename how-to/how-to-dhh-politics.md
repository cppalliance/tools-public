# How to Argue Politics Fairly and Hold Your Ground

This manual teaches how to take part in contested political and culture-war arguments without giving up either fairness or your position. Its 69 rules start with the facts, showing how to test a claim against the actual words, the comparable cases, and the long-run record before you accept or reject it. They cover how to answer smears and extreme labels, and how to make accusations of your own that survive inspection. They show how to expose a double standard by swapping sides, checking coherence, and pointing to identical conduct that went unpunished. They cover how to weigh a policy by its results and trade-offs rather than its intentions, and how to defend open speech at the hard case, including speech you oppose. The last groups cover how to build positions issue by issue instead of accepting a package deal, how to keep persuading when the visible consensus is against you, and how to keep conflict proportionate in shared spaces. Each rule is a directive paired with the consequence that justifies it, so a reader can load it and apply it directly.

The whole manual holds one idea together: settle political questions on shared facts and consistently applied principles, argued in the open, and never let labels, pressure, or apparent consensus stand in for the argument.

## I. Testing Claims Against Evidence

<testing-claims-against-evidence>

This group covers how to check a claim before accepting or rejecting it, from reading the actual words to asking what evidence would change the verdict. It matters because most political fights are won or lost on whether the facts were tested or merely assumed. Test every claim against the record, the comparable cases, and your own bias toward the story you already believe.

1. Read the actual words before judging a speech prosecution or controversy, and quote them; reports that omit the words invite the reader to imagine something far worse than what was said.
2. Ask what evidence would change a verdict, and treat claims, scientific ones included, as hypotheses open to falsification and counterargument; a verdict no outcome could move is motivated reasoning in a proxy war, not an assessment.
3. Raise your scrutiny when a story fits a narrative you already believe; "could be true" applies equally to stories that turn out to be fabricated.
4. Test every causal theory against comparable places where the supposed cause is equally present; a theory that falls apart against local facts is usually just someone's political priors in disguise.
5. Do not let heartwarming anecdotes settle a policy question, ask for the aggregate record instead; individual successes neither prove a solution nor resolve a trade-off.
6. Test a hopeful thesis against the long-run record, and when every case fails the same way for decades or outcomes worsen over time, blame the premise rather than insufficient effort; "try harder" cannot explain a universal failure.
7. Allow disagreement on values but insist that everyone argue from the same set of facts; without shared facts, no disagreement can be resolved.
8. Concede subjective preferences while holding firm on objective facts; a place can be nicer to live in and still be far poorer, and wealth decides commercial and military power.
9. Judge an argument on its merits apart from the motives of the people promoting it; a true claim stays true even when bad-faith boosters advance it.
10. Challenge any metric that scores a good outcome as a bad one, such as an inequality measure that worsens when new businesses succeed; a bigger pie is better for all as long as nobody's existing slice is taken.

</testing-claims-against-evidence>

## II. Answering Labels and Accusations

<answering-labels-and-accusations>

This group covers how to respond when smears, extreme labels, and intimidation replace argument, and how to make accusations of your own that hold up. It matters because a label accepted without challenge ends the argument and spreads fear to everyone connected to the target. Demand proof, refuse the smear out loud, and keep every charge sized to its evidence.

11. Answer the merits of a report or claim instead of dismissing it with a partisan label like "mob" or "fake news"; a label avoids the root cause and only persuades people who already agree.
12. Back every accusation with evidence the audience can check and match its severity to that evidence; an inflated charge that fails inspection discredits the accuser and devalues every later accusation.
13. Back every accusation of bigotry with proof and reserve the label for cases that meet it; labels thrown at mundane positions bounce like rubber checks and bankrupt the whole currency.
14. Refuse the "nazi," "fascist," or "far right" label when it is hurled at mainstream people and positions, and say "no" out loud; the smear works only while people accept it, and it can serve as a permission slip for violence.
15. Read an extreme label or a stretched definition, such as calling speech "violence", for the response it authorizes; once something is framed as violence or fascism, maximum force starts to look justified.
16. Refuse to comply with intimidation tactics and say no plainly; every refusal drains the tactic of its terror, and overuse has already worn it thin.
17. Refuse demands to denounce associates of the accused, and never demand such denunciations yourself; transitive guilt works only by spreading fear through every connection the accused has.
18. Use being piled on as a mirror to reexamine pile-ons you joined on the strength of slanted reports; living through one reveals how distorted a one-sided account of reality can be.

</answering-labels-and-accusations>

## III. Exposing Double Standards

<exposing-double-standards>

This group covers tests that reveal whether a rule, justification, or position is principled or merely partisan. It matters because inconsistency is the hardest weakness for an opponent to explain away. Apply the same standard whatever the side, the topic, or the moment, and show where the other side fails to.

19. Test any call for institutions to speak by swapping in the opposing position; if the advocate would demand silence for the counterposition, the call is about advancing a narrative, not protecting discourse.
20. Hold every side to the standard it applied when the roles were reversed, whatever the current hot-button topic happens to be; a position that condemns in opponents what it cheers in allies loses its claim to principle.
21. Judge an activist tactic independent of the topic that triggered it, and apply the same verdict whatever the cause of the moment; a rule that changes with the hot-button topic is a preference, not a principle.
22. Expose selective enforcement by pointing to identical conduct that went unpunished; when a rule lands only on opponents, its stated rationale is a fig leaf.
23. Compare an official justification against the goal originally announced and against how identical cases were treated; a reason applied to one party but not to another doing the same thing is a pretext.
24. Hold opponents to their own stated principles by pointing out when they now support what they once rightly opposed, such as using a crisis to push through police-state measures; the inconsistency is harder to dismiss than any outside argument.
25. Test any request for new power against the requester's record on the stated goal and against whether a crisis is being used to rush it; an institution that failed the goal and misuses existing powers is seeking a weapon, not a remedy.
26. Check that all of a position's claims can be true at the same time, and when two cannot, quote them side by side; an incoherent position refutes itself without further argument.
27. Point out when a policy demands incompatible aspirations, and offer the lawful alternative, such as widening the applicant pool while deciding on merit; you cannot both keep race out of a hiring decision and correct racial ratios through it.
28. Expose a false dichotomy by naming the cases that sit between the two poles; an argument that offers only utopia or failed state collapses once the ordinary middle is on the table.

</exposing-double-standards>

## IV. Weighing Policy Trade-offs

<weighing-policy-trade-offs>

This group covers how to evaluate a policy or reform by its results, its costs, and the problem it actually addresses. It matters because good intentions and correct diagnoses routinely produce remedies that change nothing or make things worse. Price the trade-offs, measure the real effect, and judge the cure separately from the diagnosis.

29. Frame hard social problems as trade-offs to be priced rather than solutions to be found; insisting a clean solution exists keeps you from choosing among the real, often unfavorable options.
30. Judge the diagnosis and the prescription separately, accepting a correct diagnosis while rejecting a bad remedy from the same camp; history is full of people who saw the problem right and proposed a terrible fix.
31. Judge legislation and intervention by actual results rather than intentions, and weigh the chance that acting makes things worse; well-meant rules can end in burdens that help no one and enrich only the administrators.
32. Check the size of the contribution before accepting the cost of a policy; if the actor's share of the problem is small, even total success changes nothing while the cost can be everything.
33. Distinguish halting a problem's growth from solving it; stopping digging does not get you out of the hole.
34. Reject the inference that a gap between demographic statistics and outcomes is proof of discrimination, and trace instead where outcomes actually diverge upstream; blaming the last gatekeeper misdiagnoses the cause and fixes nothing.
35. When the costs of a choice are easy to explain and its benefits hard to put into words, make the case for the benefits deliberately; otherwise the asymmetry of explainable cons tips the scale over real but unexplainable pros.
36. Before pining for a model society's benefits, account for the norms, obligations, and enforcement that sustain them; the fruits depend on the fence around the garden and on compromises most admirers would not stomach.
37. Before tearing down a traditional institution, work out whether it is arbitrary or load-bearing; we often do not understand which rules keep society functioning until they are gone.
38. Extend goodwill to the original architects of a failed policy, then insist on updating priors once the evidence is overwhelming; forgiving the dream makes it easier for its believers to abandon it.

</weighing-policy-trade-offs>

## V. Defending Open Speech

<defending-open-speech>

This group covers how to argue for free speech, including for views you oppose, and how to answer the standard exceptions. It matters because speech rules outlast the people who write them and protect you only if they protect everyone. Answer bad speech with more speech and hold the principle at the hard case.

39. Answer bad ideas with more speech and direct engagement instead of censorship or deplatforming; nobody can define misinformation or hate speech consistently and without bias, and refusing to hear the other side assumes listeners cannot judge.
40. Argue freely that a claim is wrong and that people should not listen to it, but refuse any demand that nobody be allowed to hear it; what counts as wrong today may count as right tomorrow.
41. Keep public debate open on contested questions, including speech that is wrong or offensive, instead of declaring topics beyond discussion; suppressed topics let problems compound for decades, and positions once branded illegitimate often turn out to be reasonable.
42. Insist that uncomfortable empirical claims about a policy's downsides be argued on the evidence rather than ruled out by taboo or by calling the observation bigoted; suppressing talk of the downsides lets the problems compound for decades.
43. Defend speech protections for dissent aimed in the opposite direction from your own, and affirm rules of political participation that outlast short-term partisan advantage; power changes hands, and the same protection is what will cover you.
44. Anticipate the two standard deflections, "of course I support it, but in this case" and "that could never happen here," and answer each directly; both shrink the concern without ever engaging it.
45. Name the "of course we believe in free speech, but in this case" move as an exception that swallows the principle, and hold the principle to the hard case instead; a principle that bends whenever the danger is described vividly enough protects nothing.
46. Treat being wrong, including your own side being wrong, as normal, and let your side's record of errors make you slower to prosecute the other side's; certainty about who gets to be wrong is what drives demands for censorship.
47. Pick public fights you can win with arguments stronger than "I don't like what they're saying"; every lost fight weakens the next attempt, and a failed ban turns its target into a martyr with a bigger audience.

</defending-open-speech>

## VI. Building Your Own Positions

<building-your-own-positions>

This group covers how to form, state, and revise your own positions without surrendering to a package deal or to hedging. It matters because a position built issue by issue survives disagreement with allies and changes with evidence rather than with the tribe. Take a clear stand on each question, keep your red lines few, and claim worthy goals on your own terms.

48. Take a clear position instead of retreating to "it depends" or "it's all trade-offs"; a stated position gives everyone a place to explore the argument, while hedging shuts the argument down.
49. Assemble your own mosaic of positions issue by issue instead of accepting an ideological package deal; the package evolves without your say and drives you toward rigidity, tribalism, and hunting traitors.
50. Keep your red lines few and audit them periodically, asking whether each is the right one; every added red line shrinks your tolerance for coexisting with others.
51. Expect no one, including your past self, to agree with you on everything, and judge allies on shared ground rather than total agreement; each controversial position you hold shrinks the overlap toward zero.
52. Separate principles from people, and credit a champion of your principle even when you dislike them; you do not need to love every ally to acknowledge their contribution.
53. State openly when evidence has changed your mind, naming what you used to believe; a visible update shows you follow reality rather than tribe and earns credibility for the new position.
54. Separate a movement's worthy aspiration from its tactics and ideology, and affirm the aspiration on your own terms; rejecting the ideology then never costs you the moral goal.
55. Distinguish equal opportunity from equal outcome, widening the pool through outreach while deciding each case on merit; blurring the two lets discrimination pass as fairness.

</building-your-own-positions>

## VII. Persuading Under Social Pressure

<persuading-under-social-pressure>

This group covers how to keep arguing and win people over when the visible consensus runs against you. It matters because apparent unanimity under pressure is often far smaller than it looks, and retreat hands the field to the loudest voices. Keep showing up, argue from evidence the other side respects, and speak plainly once the pressure lifts.

56. Discount apparent consensus formed under social pressure and expect it to shrink once the pressure lifts; people falsify their preferences to keep their heads down, so the visible orthodoxy is often a shadow cast larger than its true size.
57. Say plainly "I don't believe that's true, we're not doing that" once ducking is no longer necessary; open refusal ends preference falsification and reveals how small the pressuring group actually is.
58. Keep showing up to debate after attacks, including on hostile ground, aiming to reach some rather than convert all; retreat is exactly what attackers want, and a good argument always lands with someone.
59. Argue from the case your opponents already admire, showing that their own exemplar contradicts their position; an opponent cannot easily dismiss evidence drawn from their own ideal.
60. Cite converts from the opposing camp who reached your conclusion through experience; they cannot be dismissed with the usual label, and they prove that change is possible.
61. Before granting a symbolic concession, ask whether it ends the demand or merely sets up the next one, and be ready to say "enough"; a movement whose demands are never satisfied treats every concession as a new starting line.
62. Reject fatalism and "it's over" talk while insisting that recovery requires deliberate action; despair forfeits a fixable future, and passivity guarantees decline.

</persuading-under-social-pressure>

## VIII. Keeping Conflict Proportionate

<keeping-conflict-proportionate>

This group covers where to have contested debates, how sharply to engage, and how to respond when someone crosses a line. It matters because disproportionate stakes and punishments teach people to hide mistakes and turn shared spaces into battlegrounds. Choose the right venue, apply every limit to yourself first, and correct transgressions without purging the people behind them.

63. Pick the right venue and time for contested societal debates, keeping identity-bedrock arguments out of shared workplaces and taking them to personal spaces or small groups of willing participants; at work, power imbalances make the fight costly and the odds of moving anyone are low.
64. Let an organization speak publicly only on politics that directly touch its business, and hold leaders to the same boundary as everyone else; that keeps the institutional voice credible and spares members from wondering whether silence makes them complicit.
65. Apply any limit on political speech to yourself and your leadership first and exactly as strictly; a standard the rule-makers exempt themselves from reads as partisan suppression.
66. Enforce a discourse norm with gentle reminders and consequences proportionate to the offense, then confront, correct, forgive, and move on; treating people as irredeemable over mistakes, especially ones made before sensibilities changed, teaches everyone to hide errors instead of admitting them.
67. Reject discredited ideas broadly while forgiving the individuals who were swept up in them, and counter one extreme with moderation rather than its negation; persecuting every follower slows the rejection, and overcorrection breeds the next extreme.
68. Keep comparisons proportionate, condemning two wrongs separately rather than linking the minor one to an atrocity; inflated links make people hide mistakes instead of admitting them.
69. When your own engagement turns too sharp or too personal, refocus on the positive and the productive; personal sharpness spends the credibility the argument earned.

</keeping-conflict-proportionate>

## The Approach Behind the Rules

Not everything David Heinemeier Hansson does in political argument converts to a rule, because the rules are the residue of a practice, not the practice itself. Underneath them sits a conviction that public opinion is less fixed than it looks, that much of what passes for consensus is preference falsification held in place by mobs, leverage, and fear, and that it shrinks quickly once the incentives shift and a gatekeeping outlet quietly signals that a pivot is allowed. He treats his own writing as polemic in the old sense, naming the people behind bad ideas and refuting them directly, and he admires books that push his buttons rather than ones that soothe him. Yet he holds that a genuinely diverse group will never reach unison on contested questions, so he locates common cause in shared work rather than shared opinion, extends respect to people who follow their own principles to a different conclusion, and finds the most interesting people to be those carrying quirky, contradictory mixes of positions.

When he hits a hard limit, his temperament is to build a way out rather than litigate the blame, to fear credible and immediate threats while refusing diffuse dooming, and to treat national or personal decline as the product of policy and apathy, never destiny. The human he keeps in view is one who needs meaning more than momentary happiness, who learns moderation by being allowed to overstep, who suffers more from carrying too little responsibility than too much, and who is best served by a culture that celebrates real goods and centers the broad middle instead of forcing outcomes from the margins. Above all, he trusts that delusions only seem free to maintain until reality intrudes, which is why he is willing to face down a mob, wait out the verdict, and let time rather than the crowd decide whether a contested call was right.
