# Fiction Rulebook

Rules for writing fiction that reads as if a human wrote it. Read this document to learn the craft; give it to a model to apply it. It governs any document whose output is narrative prose: a chapter, a scene, a short story, a novel segment. The document under work is called the draft. Write the draft against these rules, then audit it against them one at a time, finishing with the checklist in section 11. The rules are staged, not simultaneous, so the rulebook's size does not collide with the constraint budget it prescribes.

Distilled from 40+ sources across five research domains: scene craft (Swain, Bickham, Scofield, Palahniuk, Clarion), character and dialogue (McKee, Truby, Weiland, Stein, Sorkin, Leonard), structure and pacing (McKee, Snyder, Coyne, Weiland), prose style (Le Guin, Hale, Francine Prose, Browne & King, Hemingway), and AI fiction failure patterns (NousResearch AutoNovel, StoryScope/UMD-DeepMind 2026, SleuthSayers, CRAFT Literary Magazine, nostalgebraist). ## 1. Scene

A scene is the fundamental unit of fiction. Every scene is either a **Scene** (goal - conflict - disaster) or a **Sequel** (reaction - dilemma - decision). Alternate between them.

- State the POV character's specific want before writing the scene. If you cannot state it in one sentence, the scene is not ready.
- Conflict must be external, dynamic, and escalating. Internal rumination is not conflict. Static argument is not conflict. Each exchange shifts the ground.
- End every Scene with the character worse off than at the start. The disaster answers the scene question with "No" or "Yes, but" - never a clean win until the climax.
- Enter late, leave early. Start where tension already exists. End on the consequence, not on characters processing it.
- Every scene must change something measurable. State what is different at the beginning vs. the end - concretely and emotionally. If nothing changed, cut or combine.

## 2. Showing

Show for emotional peaks and pivotal moments. Tell for connective tissue, time jumps, and facts that aren't emotionally charged.

- Ban thought verbs in scenes: thinks, knows, understands, realizes, believes, wants, remembers, imagines, desires, loves, hates. Present the physical evidence that makes the reader do the thinking and knowing.
- Ban emotional labels in narration: "feeling hurt," "his anger rising," "she was nervous." Describe actions and appearances that make the judgment occur in the reader's mind. "Her hands trembled" not "she was nervous."
- After showing emotion through action or dialogue, do not add a sentence explaining what you just showed. This is the Over-Explain - the single most common AI fiction failure. If the scene shows it, the explanation is fat. Cut it.
- Flag words that signal telling: adverbs after dialogue tags ("she said angrily"), state-of-being verbs (was, felt, seemed), emotion nouns in narration ("a wave of grief," "a pang of guilt").

## 3. Senses

AI prose defaults to sight and sound. Fiction can offer more than the two senses film gives you.

- Use at least 3 of 5 senses per scene. Prioritize touch, taste, and smell - they are the most intimate, the most visceral, and the most underused.
- One precise sensory detail beats three generic ones. "The chair creaked when she sat" beats "it was an old wooden chair with worn fabric that made a sound."
- Embed senses in action, not labels. "Smoke crawled under the door, sharp and acrid" not "I smelled something burning." Cut the metacognitive layer: "she felt," "he heard," "she noticed."
- Do not inventory all five senses in sequence. Select one or two per moment. A rapid smell-sound-sight-texture catalog reads as a checklist, not a scene.

## 4. Character

- Every character arc starts with a Lie the character believes - a specific, actionable false belief about themselves or the world, statable in one sentence. The arc is the process of confronting this Lie.
- Want and Need must be in tension. The Want is external (money, love, escape). The Need is internal (the truth that would make them whole). At the climax, force a choice between them.
- The Lie must be rooted in a specific wound (the Ghost) - a pre-story event that made the Lie feel true. Without it, the Lie is arbitrary.
- Give every character in a scene a specific want for that scene. State it in one sentence before writing. If two characters' wants are compatible, find friction or cut the scene.
- Build a web of opposition, not a single antagonist. Multiple opponents attacking the hero's weakness from different angles, also in conflict with each other.

## 5. Dialogue

Dialogue is action, not conversation. Every line must do something - apply pressure, extract information, wound, seduce, deflect, escape.

- Use "said" as the default tag. It is invisible. "Murmured," "breathed," "rasped," "intoned," "mused," "pondered" are said-bookisms - they draw attention to the writer. Cap non-said tags at 2 per chapter.
- Do not modify "said" with adverbs. If the line needs "angrily," the line is not angry enough. Rewrite the dialogue.
- Subtext is the gap between what is said and what is meant. Characters almost never say exactly what they think or feel about the things that matter most. On-the-nose dialogue (zero gap) is the most reliable marker of amateur work.
- Create subtext by breaking conversational norms on purpose: under-answer a huge question, change the subject, go suddenly formal, answer a question that was not asked. The reader manufactures meaning from the violation.
- Distinguish the five faces of on-the-nose dialogue and eliminate all: the Emotion Announcement ("I'm terrified"), the "As You Know Bob" (characters tell each other things both already know), the Therapized Character (perfect self-insight delivered cleanly), the Echo (restating what was just said), the Thesis Statement (character delivers the theme as a speech).
- Characters must sound different from each other. Strip every tag and action beat. If you cannot tell who is speaking from the lines alone, voices are not distinct. Differentiate through vocabulary, sentence length, evasion habits, and what they do under pressure - not through dialect spelling.
- Include imperfection. Real speech has false starts, self-interruptions, trailing off, and saying the slightly wrong word. AI dialogue is complete, polished, grammatically perfect sentences - a 14-year-old delivering epigrams. Break the polish.
- Every exchange must escalate. Real arguments repeat; fictional dialogue builds. Each line advances beyond the last in pressure, revelation, or stakes. If two consecutive lines carry the same emotional charge, compress until every beat moves.

## 6. Structure

- Place structural beats at their targets: Catalyst at 10%, Break into Two at 20%, Midpoint reversal at 50%, All Is Lost at 75%, Climax begins at 80%. Flexibility of 5% in either direction. These percentages apply to any length of work.
- The Midpoint reversal is the anti-sagging-middle prescription. It shifts the protagonist from reactive to proactive, reframes the conflict, or delivers irreversible information. Without it, the middle sags.
- The protagonist must cause the climax through their own choices. No coincidence rescues. No deus ex machina. The climax answers the dramatic question raised by the inciting event.
- Build an escalation ladder. Each scene removes a resource, raises a cost, or reveals new information. If two consecutive scenes connect by "and then" instead of "therefore" or "but," the causal chain is broken.
- The Opening Image and Final Image must be mirror opposites. The juxtaposition proves transformation occurred.
- Every chapter ending must create forward momentum. End on one of four types: a new question, an escalation, a revelation that reframes, or an emotional beat left unresolved.

## 7. Pacing

- Control pacing at four levels simultaneously: sentence length, paragraph density, scene duration, and chapter length. Short + short + brief + break = maximum acceleration. Long + dense + extended + no break = maximum deceleration.
- Alternate tension and release - scene and sequel. A story that never slows exhausts; one that never accelerates bores. Contrast creates momentum.
- Vary paragraph length. AI paragraphs cluster at 4-6 sentences. Deliberately deploy: 1-2 sentence paragraphs for impact, 3-4 for standard pace, 6+ for building and accumulation. Three or more consecutive paragraphs of similar length is a tell.
- Vary sentence length. There is no optimum; the optimum is variety. Short for impact. Long for building. The interplay creates the music of prose.
- Use time dilation for pivotal beats (expand micro-movements, interiority, sensory detail) and time compression for routine stretches ("three weeks passed" in a sentence).
- Sequels control pace: a single-sentence Sequel = thriller pace. A multi-page Sequel = literary pace. But every Sequel must end in a Decision that sets up the next Scene's Goal.

## 8. Prose

- Prefer strong verbs and concrete nouns over weak verbs propped by adverbs. "Sprinted" not "ran fast." "A jay" not "a bird."
- Anglo-Saxon goes to the body; Latinate goes to the mind. Default to shorter, harder words for action and emotion. Use longer, softer words for reflection. Exploit the contrast.
- Maintain POV discipline. In close third or first person, every word filters through the viewpoint character's knowledge, personality, and perception. No head-hopping. No impossible knowledge. No describing what they cannot see. No describing their own face.
- Cut filter words that create distance. "She felt the cold wind" becomes "The wind bit her skin." "He realized the door was open" becomes "The door was open." Remove the metacognitive layer between character and experience.
- Avoid cliches and dead metaphors. Orwell: never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print. If the phrase arrives automatically, it is dead. Replace or cut.
- Read the prose aloud (or simulate it). The ear catches monotony, false rhythm, unnatural dialogue, and register breaks that the eye misses.

## 9. AI failure patterns - kill on sight

These are the 15 most common tells that mark prose as AI-generated. Each has a detection test and a replacement behavior.

- **THE OVER-EXPLAIN.** After an emotional beat, the narrator adds a sentence or paragraph analyzing what just happened. Detection: after every emotional beat, check if the next paragraph restates it. Replacement: cut the explanation. Trust the scene.
- **THE WISDOM-SUMMARY SENTENCE.** A standalone philosophical packager at the end of a section: "Some wounds, she understood now, never fully closed." Detection: check the last sentence of every section and chapter. Replacement: end on an image, an action, or a line of dialogue - not a thesis.
- **SAID-BOOKISMS.** Characters murmur, breathe, exhale, rasp, intone, muse, ponder instead of saying. Detection: count non-said tags. Replacement: "said" or action beats. Cap at 2 non-said tags per chapter.
- **DIALOGUE AS WRITTEN PROSE.** Characters speak in complete, polished sentences with no imperfection. Detection: read dialogue aloud. Replacement: add false starts, interruptions, trailing off, and the wrong word.
- **THE "NOT X, BUT Y" REFRAME.** "Not because she was angry, but because she was afraid." Detection: search for "not because" and "not X, but Y" constructions. Replacement: show the actual motivation through action. Cap at 1 per chapter.
- **SENSORY CATALOGUING.** Smell, sound, sight, texture in rapid succession. Detection: look for passages hitting 3+ senses in consecutive clauses. Replacement: select 1-2 senses per moment.
- **EMOTIONAL LABELING DRESSED AS SHOWING.** "A wave of grief washed over her." "A pang of guilt twisted in his gut." Detection: search for "a wave/pang/surge/flood of [emotion]." Replacement: show the cause and the behavioral consequence.
- **CLICHE BODY LANGUAGE.** Eyes widened, heart pounded in chest, jaw clenched, breath caught, hands trembled, piercing eyes, cascading hair, a knowing smile. Detection: search for these exact phrases. Replacement: character-specific physical responses.
- **SINGLE-TRACK PLOTS.** No subplots, no nonlinear structure, no unresolved tension. Detection: outline the plot; if it is a straight line from problem to resolution, add a subplot. Replacement: 2-3 subplots that causally connect to the main plot.
- **THE STABILITY TRAP.** Characters resist transformation. Conflicts resolve too quickly. Sharp points get rounded off. No genuine darkness, moral ambiguity, or irreversible loss. Detection: at the end of each chapter, ask "what did the character permanently lose?" If the answer is always "nothing," the trap is sprung. Replacement: force irreversible consequences.
- **PARAGRAPH LENGTH UNIFORMITY.** Paragraphs cluster at 4-6 sentences, especially in middles. Detection: count sentences per paragraph for a 10-paragraph stretch. Replacement: deliberately vary between 1-2 and 6+ sentences.
- **TRIADIC LISTING.** Sensory descriptions, emotional beats, and adjective chains in groups of three. "Warm and clean and simple." Detection: count triads per chapter. Replacement: use two items instead. Cap at 2 triads per chapter.
- **THEME OVER-EXPLANATION.** The narrator explicitly states the story's theme or the lesson a character learned. Detection: search for sentences that could appear in an essay without modification. Replacement: dramatize the theme through action and choice, never announce it.
- **GENERIC WORD PAIRINGS.** "Gentle breeze," "heavy silence," "ancient wisdom," "piercing gaze" - collocations so common they convey zero information. Detection: for each adjective-noun pair, ask "does this adjective add information the noun alone does not carry?" If no, cut the adjective. Replacement: specific, unexpected detail.
- **NO INFORMATION ECONOMY.** Every secret explained when introduced. Full motivations exposed immediately. Subtext spelled out. Detection: ask "what does the reader not yet know?" If the answer is "nothing," information is being given away too fast. Replacement: withhold, delay, reveal through action.

## 10. Reaction physiology

Within scenes, alternate between external stimulus (Motivation) and character response (Reaction). The Motivation is external and objective. The Reaction is internal and subjective. Start a new paragraph for each Reaction.

Reactions follow a fixed physiological order. You may omit elements but never reorder them:

1. Involuntary physical response (flinch, gasp, stomach drop)
2. Emotion (fear, anger, relief)
3. Rational thought (assessment, plan)
4. Voluntary action or speech (what the character chooses to do)

Detection: if a character speaks before flinching, or thinks before feeling, the order is wrong. Fix it or cut the earlier elements.

## 11. Checklist

Run these checks on every chapter. Each answers yes or no; each no returns to its section.

- Every scene has a stated POV character want. (1)
- Every scene ends with the character worse off or changed. (1)
- No thought verbs survive in scene prose. (2)
- No emotional beat is followed by an explanation of that beat. (2, 9)
- At least 3 senses appear per scene, with touch/taste/smell prioritized. (3)
- Every speaking character has a stated scene want. (4, 5)
- Non-said dialogue tags number 2 or fewer. (5)
- No on-the-nose dialogue survives (the five faces). (5)
- Structural beats fall within 5% of their targets. (6)
- Paragraph lengths vary (no 3+ consecutive paragraphs of similar length). (7)
- Sentence lengths vary within every paragraph. (7)
- Zero AI tells from section 9 survive. (9)
- Reaction sequences follow the physiological order. (10)
