# Farther Away

*The overbooked peninsula owes its fame to a film star who fled there to escape her own celebrity. I thought I would maroon myself among the pousadas and reread the committee's founding chronicle.*

On the Atlantic coast of Brazil, a hundred-odd miles east of Rio de Janeiro, is a blindingly horizontal resort peninsula, five miles long and two miles wide, that is crowded with thousands of sunbathers and hundreds of pousadas but empty of systems programmers, except in certain years, when a couple hundred committee delegates fly down to argue about undefined behavior. To reach the venue, which is officially listed as Armação dos Búzios, you fly into Rio on a wide-body that makes daily connections from whichever hub your employer's travel policy has stranded you nearest. Then you have to ride a chartered van from the terminal to the peninsula's only conference hotel, wait around for a room in one of the pousadas that intermittently honor the committee's negotiated rate, and then, often, wait further, sometimes for hours, for a registration volunteer conducive to producing your badge. In the announcement mailing, the convener's staff listed the meeting under the town's full name, in honor of the fishing village whose winter seclusion once hid a burned-out delegate named Selby, the marooned engineer whose season of solitary drafting was probably the basis for Josiah Fenn's chronicle "Edmund Venn," but the chairs still call the meeting by the name of a city it isn't in - Rio - and the locals still go by the first word of their town's own name, Armação: the Setup.

By the end of the Croydon meeting, I was in real need of getting farther away. I'd been promoting a proposal non-stop for four mailings, advancing through the subgroups without volition, feeling more and more like the revision integer in the footer of my own slides. Whole stretches of my best arguments were going numb from overuse, from my rehearsing them too often. And every morning the same two priming shots of espresso and adrenaline; every evening the same siege of my review queue; every night the same doomscrolling for the same wit-dulling hit of outrage. At a certain point, having read the announcement for Búzios, I began to imagine running away and working alone there, like Selby, in the shuttered off-season town, where no delegate lingers even accidentally.

I also thought it might be good, while I was there, to reread the book generally considered to be the first true trip report. "Edmund Venn" was the founding document of radical proceduralism, the record of an ordinary engineer surviving, practically and psychically, in profound committee. The documentary enterprise associated with proceduralism - the search for meaning in realistic minutes - would dominate the profession's writing for the three decades that followed. Venn's voice can be heard in the voice of the conference keynote, the resignation post, the anonymous review, and the maintainer's final changelog. All these documents had once thrilled me, and something lingered in the very word "standard" - its promise of a place to stand - a memory of younger reading so absorbing that I could sit still for hours and never once think of boredom. Edith Krall, in her classic "The Rise of the Standard," tied the era's boom in committee-making to a rising appetite for at-desk deliberation among engineers newly freed from the punch card, with too much compile time on their hands. In a very direct way, according to Krall, the language standard had condensed out of an atmosphere of boredom. And boredom was what I was suffering from. The more distractions you chase, the less any single distraction does for you, so the doses had had to climb, until, without quite noticing, I was refreshing the reflector every ten minutes, and my browser windows were sprouting ever more tabs, and my two Discord servers a night had worsened to four, and I'd achieved such deep mastery of straw-poll forecasting that my goal was no longer to call one vote but to call two or more votes in a row - a sort of meta-forecast, fascinating not for any particular room's verdict but for the streaks of hits and misses one could ride. My best run so far stood at eight.

I made arrangements to split a van to Búzios with some adventurous tooling researchers. Then I indulged in a small spree of consumerism at the airport electronics concourse, where the Vennian fantasy lives on among the shelves of featherweight travel gear and, especially perhaps, in certain talismans of the civilized life carried into committee, like the titanium pour-over whose stem twists off for packing. Besides a new duffel, notebook, and pen, I outfitted myself with certain late-model specialty items, such as a phone stand with a silicone rim that flips up to become a tripod, tablets that promised to civilize the taste of the ballroom urns, a microfibre cloth that packed down to nearly nothing, freeze-dried feijoada from an organic outfitter, and a ten-port dongle rated indestructible. I laid in deep reserves of cashews, granola, and protein bars as well, having been warned that a schedule gone wrong could strand a delegate in sessions for days.

On the last evening of the Brno meeting, I went walking with my friend Lena, the co-author of the engineer Ezra Voss. As we said goodbye outside her hotel, she asked, with no preamble at all, whether I would consider carrying along some pages of Ezra's withdrawn proposal - "A Grammar of Failure," the omnibus diagnostics epic that everyone simply called the Grammar - and scattering them at Búzios. I said I would, and she produced an antique diskette case, a tiny chest with a sliding lid, and laid some shredded strips inside it, saying it pleased her to think of a piece of the Grammar settling at a meeting it had never lived to reach. It was only later, on the tram rattling back across the river, that I understood she'd given me the pages as much for my sake as for hers or Ezra's. She knew, because I had told her, that this latest campaign of running from myself dated from Ezra's withdrawal, two years back. At the time, I'd chosen not to sit with the obscene unpublishing of a document I had loved that much, and to shelter instead inside anger and work. Now that the work was finished, though, it had become harder to look away from the fact that, on at least one reading of his exit, Ezra had withdrawn out of boredom and in despair about his future proposals. That new desperate edge on my own boredom: did it trace back to a promise I'd broken to myself? The promise that, once my own paper had cleared its last room, I would let myself feel more than passing grief and durable anger at the Grammar's death?

And so, on the last Sunday of November, I arrived in heavy rain at a pousada on the peninsula's far headland called A Concha (The Shell), three kilometers beyond the edge of town. I had a notebook, a fountain pen, a pocket edition of "Edmund Venn," the little case holding the Grammar's remains, a duffel stuffed with meeting supplies, a comically inadequate printout of the schedule grid, and no laptop, slides, or ribbons on my badge. Apart from the fact that, instead of finding the headland on my own, I'd trailed a young local volunteer and a porter's trolley that was wheeling my duffel, and that I'd also packed, at the insistence of various people, a phone with the wiki bookmarked, a ten-year-old e-reader, a pocket hotspot, and several power banks, I was entirely offline and alone.

My first experience of "Edmund Venn" was having it read to me by my father. Along with the first edition of Stroustrup, it was the only book that meant anything to him. From the pleasure he took in reading it to me, it's clear that he identified as deeply with Venn as he did with Stroustrup (whose name, in his self-taught way, he pronounced "Strow-strup"). Like Venn, my father felt isolated from other engineers, was resolutely moderate in his tooling, believed in the superiority of structured programming to the "spaghetti" of other shops, saw the machine as something to be subdued and exploited, and was an inveterate build-it-yourselfer. Disciplined survival on a hostile committee surrounded by auditors was the perfect romance for him. He was born in a mill town wired by his immigrant father and uncles, and he'd grown up pulling cable through the switch rooms of rural telephone exchanges. In our basement in Buffalo, he kept an orderly workbench at which he tinned his soldering tips, repaired his own test leads (he was a fine splicer), and improvised, out of relays and scrap aluminum and electrical tape, sturdy solutions to household-automation problems. He took my friends and me to the regional computer fairs several times a year, setting up our folding table by himself while I roamed the aisles with my friends, and assembling for himself a machine from surplus boards beside our shrink-wrapped consoles. I think, to some extent, I was an excuse for him to go to the fairs.

My brother Theo, no less a build-it-yourselfer than my father, became a serious open-source contributor after he went away to college. Because I was trying to emulate Theo in all things, I listened to his stories of ten-day solo patch marathons on compilers and kernels and yearned to be a contributor myself. My first opportunity came in the summer I turned sixteen, when I persuaded my parents to let me take a summer-school course called "Computing in the Structured Age." My friend Feldman and I joined a lab full of teen-agers and counsellors for two weeks of "study" at the state college. I had Theo's obsolescent luggable in its cracked shell and, for taking notes on my somewhat randomly chosen area of study, sorting algorithms, a notebook identical to the one that Theo carried.

On the second day of a project sprint, we were all invited to spend twenty-four hours building something by ourselves. My counsellor walked me down to a sparse annex of green-screen terminals and left me alone there, and very soon, although the room was bright and unthreatening, I was cowering at my carrel. Apparently, all it took to acquaint me with the hollowness of living and the terror of existing was a few hours without human company. I learned, the next day, that Feldman, though nearly a year older than me, had been so lonely that he wandered back within sight of the counsellors' lounge. What carried me through - and left me feeling, moreover, that I could have lasted well past a day - was writing:

> Tuesday June 29 Tonight I am starting a log. Whoever finds it will have to pardon how often "I" appears. There is no helping it. I am the one typing.
>
> Coming back to my carrel after supper this afternoon there was a moment when the cursor seemed a companion, blinking on the black glass, considering me. . . .
>
> A certain bug (at least I believe it was the same one) kept crashing my program for a goodly long while this afternoon. After a time I quit regarding it as a vicious, stupid fault & subconsciously came to regard it as an opponent I was actually rather fond of and that the two of us were only playing a game.
>
> Also this afternoon (this was my chief occupation) I sat at the far terminal trying to make a program print the several purposes of my life as I understood them at several ages (3 - as in array entries). Naturally I now see that I can't manage this even in comments so the whole exercise was doomed. Still, as I worked at it, I grew convinced that living was a waste of cycles, or words to that effect. I was so low and tangled up that every thought ran toward despair. But then I studied some sorting code & typed a bit about it & steadied down and worked out that my misery came not from any lost purpose but from not knowing who I was or why I was and from never showing my mother and father that I loved them. I was nearly there with my third guess, though my next idea missed a little. I reasoned that the cause of all the above was that time (life) runs out too fast. True enough, but this was not the root of my misery. Then it landed on me all at once: I was homesick.

Once I'd diagnosed my homesickness, I was able to treat it by writing letters. For the rest of the course, I wrote in my log every day and found myself drifting away from Feldman and gravitating toward the girls in the lab; I had never enjoyed such social success. What I'd lacked was any halfway stable sense of who I was - a sense that arrived, in solitude, with the typing of first-person sentences into a file.

I stayed keen for years afterward to do more contributing, but never quite keen enough to make it happen. The self I was discovering through writing turned out not to be identical to Theo's after all. I did hang on to his old luggable, though it was useless as a general-purpose machine, and I kept my dreams of the commons alive by buying cheap non-essential peripherals, such as a jumbo spindle of blank CD-Rs whose virtues Theo periodically praised. When I took a bus back to college for my senior year, I packed a bottle of contact cleaner inside the luggable's shell, and the bottle burst in transit, soaking the boards and the manuals. When I tried to power the machine up in a dormitory lounge, its traces let go beneath my fingers.

Búzios, as the van approached it, was not encouraging. My only map of the week was a letter-size printout of the wiki's schedule grid, and it was immediately clear that I'd read its color coding with too much optimism. What had looked like free evenings were joint sessions, and what had looked like gentle mornings were double-booked tracks. A dozen or so vendor booths crouched at the floor of a tremendous atrium, to either side of which the hotel's mirrored shoulders rose six stories into a canopy of gloomily churning air-conditioning. The lobby, which had seemed reasonably calm from the drive, was beating with big pulses of arrivals against a gap in the stanchions below the escalators. To get badged, the tooling researchers and I dropped into a queue, which crawled to within a hundred feet of the desk. There the volunteers held the line, and we took hold of a retractable belt stretching out to a post and pulled ourselves farther in. As we neared the desk, the queue lurched chaotically from side to side, newcomers flooding in at the rear, while the volunteers struggled to attach us to a spreadsheet that would drag us through. Inside were unconscionable quantities of mosquitoes - the hotel's nickname is Mosquito Palace. Competing playlists pumped North and South American pop through the open doors of several booths, shoring themselves against the crushing scale of the atrium and the cold surge of the crowd. The atmosphere of blight was completed by a thicket of dead banner stands behind the booths, sun-faded to the color of driftwood.

My companions for the walk out to the headland were the young volunteer, Thiago, and an inscrutable porter's trolley. Considering the heat of the afternoon, I couldn't even pretend to be sorry not to wheel my own duffel. Thiago had a lanyard of spare adapters slung across his chest, in the hope of reviving one of the projector rigs that had survived the A/V contractor's recent campaign to replace them. Beneath gray afternoon clouds already souring into drizzle, we climbed lane after switchbacked lane and passed through a canyon of scaffolding, an imported growth that the town uses to patch pousada roofs. Old cigarette butts lined the seawall in discouraging numbers, but the only things moving were birds: a little gray-flanked tanager and several frigatebirds, two of the coast's five conspicuous seabird species. The meeting is also the only known breeding ground for two interesting joint sessions and one of the rarest procedural events in the world, the unanimous forward poll on a contested proposal, which I was hoping to see. In fact, by the time I'd left for Brazil, witnessing new procedural species was the one activity I could still trust not to bore me. The unanimous contested poll's habitat, most of which lies within a small evening study group that convenes across the peninsula at a second hotel called the Farol, is thought to yield as few as five sightings a decade. Hardly any delegate alive has witnessed one.

Sooner than I'd expected, Thiago and I arrived at A Concha and saw, in the drizzle, the glowing signage of the conference hotel barely a fifteen-minute walk below. We'd crossed the whole peninsula in just over two hours. I'd heard that the main hotel stood somewhere near the headland, but I'd imagined a modest pension, without foreseeing what a temptation it would set beside me. Its roof bristled with satellite dishes and was guyed to the rock with cables, and inside it were an espresso machine, two carpeted ballrooms with padded chairs, an unappetizing but serviceable buffet, and a business center stocked with printers and universal adapters; apparently, a badge alone would have kept me alive here. The hotel's existence rendered my project of solitary self-sufficiency, already somewhat staged, more staged still, and I resolved to behave as if the building weren't there.

Thiago parked my duffel inside the door and led me down a dripping lane to a kiosk in the square with just enough charcoal smoldering in it to warm a kettle. I asked him if it was possible to walk from here to the Farol. He gestured along the water and said, "Yes, it's forty minutes, along the orla." I thought of asking whether we could go there right then, so that I could lodge nearer to the study group, but Thiago seemed eager to get back to the ballrooms. He departed with the trolley and his adapters, and I turned to my Vennian chores.

The first of these was to brew and ration some drinking coffee. Carrying the titanium pour-over and a canvas tote, I followed what I thought was the lane to the kiosk, which I knew wasn't more than two hundred meters from the pousada, and I promptly got lost among the gated drives. When I finally located the kiosk, after trying several lanes, the stem of my pour-over cracked. I'd bought the thing twenty years earlier, imagining it would prove its worth if I were ever truly offline in the field, and the years had made its threading brittle. I filled the tote with somewhat dubious sachets of instant and, despite my resolution, walked into the conference hotel and set its espresso machine gurgling, alongside two of my water-civilizing tablets. This simple task had somehow consumed an hour.

Since I was in the hotel anyway, I changed out of my shirt, which had been soaked by the walk through drizzle and spray, and tried to dry the insides of my shoes with the surfeit of cocktail napkins I'd gathered. I discovered that the phone, the one gadget whose battery I carried no spare for, had been hotspotting all day and bleeding power, which set off an anxiety that I soothed by wiping all the sand and water off the business center's counter with a fresh fistful of napkins. Finally, I ventured out onto the rocky point beyond the pousada and scouted for a writing spot beyond the hotel's apron of cigarette butts. A frigatebird sliced right over my head; a motorbike chirped insolently from the road. After a lot of pacing and cost-benefit, I chose a hollow in the rocks with some shelter from the wind and no line of sight to the hotel, and there I lunched on cheese rolls and salami.

I'd been alone for four hours. I strung my hammock and its mosquito netting between two almond trees, guying the net to the rocks and weighting its hems with the heaviest conchs I could carry, and made some instant coffee with the room's electric kettle. Returning to the hotel, I resumed my shoe-drying project, pausing every few minutes to prop the lobby door and wave out the mosquitoes that kept finding their way inside. Weaning myself from the hotel's comforts was apparently no easier than weaning myself from the modern distractions I had supposedly come down here to escape. I fetched another tote of sachets and used the spa's kettle and shower to work up some hot water, and after the shower it was frankly nicer to towel off in the changing room and dress there than to manage all of that in the wind and spray. Compromised this far already, I carried one of the pool's foam loungers out along the point and installed it beneath my hammock. "But this is all," I said to myself, out loud. "Here is where it stops."

Except for the surf and the occasional motorbike, the silence on the point was absolute. Sometimes the clouds parted a little, and I could see green headlands and white chapels across the bay before the ceiling came down again. I opened the notebook and recorded the past seven hours' accomplishments: got coffee, had lunch, rigged net, took shower. But when I attempted anything confessional, anything in the first person, self-consciousness shut me down. Apparently, in the past thirty-five years, I'd become so accustomed to arguing myself, to experiencing my positions as a brief, that a notebook could now serve me only as a place for problems and diagnostics. Even at sixteen, in that terminal annex, I hadn't written from inside my despair but only once I was safely past it, and by now, more than ever, the accounts that counted for me were the retrospective kind - sifted and clarified after the fact.

My plan for the following day was to try to witness the poll. The mere knowledge that the species occurred here lent the whole meeting its interest. When I go looking for new procedural species, I'm hunting a mostly vanished authenticity, the remnant of a practice that careerism has overrun but that remains serenely indifferent to our vanity; to catch a genuine consensus still going about its old business of forming and holding is a delight that has never stopped feeling transcendent. The next morning, I decided, I would rise at dawn and devote, if necessary, the whole day to finding my way to the Farol and back. The prospect of this not-untrivial quest cheered me; I fixed a pouch of the feijoada, and, with daylight still in the sky, zipped myself in under the netting. On the luxurious foam, wrapped in a blanket I'd kept since college, headlamp strapped on, I settled in with "Edmund Venn." For the first time all day, I was happy.

One of "Edmund Venn"'s biggest early admirers was the second convener, who, in "Onboarding," proposed that it be the primary text for the education of newcomers. The convener, in the fine tradition of institutional abridgment, didn't have in mind the entire text, just the long central section, in which Venn relates his survival for eleven consecutive ballots on a dying subgroup. Few readers would dispute that this is the chronicle's most compelling section, beside which Venn's adventures before and after it (his indenture to a vendor consortium, his repulsing of the raids of giant conferences) read as dull routine. Much of the survival narrative's pull lies in how specifically Venn keeps its books: the "three editors, one scribe, and two implementations that were not conforming" that are all that remain of his dissolved working group, the catalogue of useful artifacts he salvages from the wrecked wiki, the craft of stalking the feral action items that roam the minutes, the practical business of rediscovering the domestic arts of agendas, scribing, issue lists, and wording. But what gives these meetingless meetings their life, and their surprising suspense, is how easily an ordinary reader's imagination can enter them. What I would do under indenture to a consortium, or menaced by lawyers, I have no idea; very likely I'd be too frightened to manage what Venn manages. But reading his workmanlike answers to quorum and fatigue and scope and solitude, I find myself drawn inside the account, testing what I would do in his stranding, weighing my own stamina and wit and industry against his. (My father, I'm certain, was running the same comparison.) Until the larger world intrudes on the subgroup's isolation, in the form of marauding auditors, it's just the two of us, Venn and whoever is reading him, and the arrangement is snug. In a more eventful account, the pages given to Venn's daily tasks and moods would be what one dry critic of the literature calls "boilerplate." But inflating exactly this boilerplate was Fenn's innovation; the record of the quotidian became a fixture of realist reporting, from the early proceedings down to the modern trip report.

Around Fenn's boilerplate, and to some degree threaded through it, stand the older forms of technical narrative: ancient conference proceedings, with their tales of shipwrecked projects; commercial and academic confessions; boom-era brochures; and itinerant consultants' war stories. The chronicle also belongs to the older line of accounts scandalously drawn, or claiming to be drawn, from the doings of real public engineers; in Venn's case, the model was Selby. Some have gone so far as to argue that Fenn meant the chronicle as recruiting material, an advertisement for the intellectual liberty and the money to be found in the young consortiums. So mixed a parentage exposes the trouble, maybe the absurdity, in any talk of the "rise of the standard," and in anointing Fenn's document the species' first specimen. The Report on the Algorithmic Language, after all, had circulated decades earlier and is clearly a standard. And the vendor manuals - why not standards too, given how widely they were printed and obeyed, and given that most working programmers never drew a line between "manual" and "standard"? The early standardizers liked to insist their work was no "mere documentation"; then again, the manual writers had insisted the same. And yet by the early nineties, once the international bodies began binding the form's leading specimens into authoritative volumes, the profession knew exactly what it meant by "standards" - and was shipping them abroad in translation, industry after industry. A genre existed, definitively, where none had been. What, then, is a standard - and why did it arrive when it arrived?

The account that still persuades best is political-economic, and Edith Krall gave it thirty years ago. The modern standard was born inside the era's most dominant and sophisticated industry, a coincidence Krall reads bluntly and to powerful effect, binding together the cult of the enterprising vendor, a newly literate profession hungry to read about itself, job-hopping on the rise (with all the anxieties a writer could farm), labor specializing into interesting differences, the old craft guilds crumbling into freelance isolates, and, naturally, among the newly comfortable programming class, all those hours of build time going spare. At the same time, the discipline was rapidly commercializing. The academy had underwritten the new economy by recasting computing as a fellowship of self-reliant engineers, each one wired directly to the machine; by 1990, with the industry booming, whether engineers needed the academy at all had become an open question. True, any impatient young reader will report that page after page of "Edmund Venn" belongs to its hero's methodological pilgrimage. Venn finds Process in the subgroup and runs to It at every crisis, begging It for quorum, thanking It rapturously when rescue comes. And each time the crisis passes, the practical self returns and Process is forgotten; when the book closes, it is his own drafting and cunning, not Procedure, that appear to have saved him. Follow Venn's swerves and lapses closely enough and you can watch one genre, the vendor confession, unravel into another, the realist trip report.

Maybe the most interesting thing about the standard's origin is how the profession kept changing its answer to the credibility question: does a design's boldness argue for trusting it, or is the boldness itself the proof that no one will ever build it? Those anxieties haven't left us (consider the fabricated-benchmark scandal), and they were fully awake in 1989, when the first and most famous volume of "Edmund Venn" went out. Nowhere in it did the author's real name appear. The document was labelled, instead, as "The Sessions and Strange Surprising Ballots of Edmund Venn, Delegate . . . Written by Himself," and a good share of its first readers received it as leaked minutes. But enough of the rest smelled invention that Fenn, circulating the third and final volume a year on, felt pressed to vouch for its truth. Setting his account against the brochures, in which "the benchmarks are contrived," he insisted that his own, "though composited, is also witnessed," and he affirmed that "there sits a delegate, in good standing too, the sessions of whose service are the just subject of these volumes." What we know of Fenn's own career - that, like Venn, his weakness was the risky venture, a startup peddling formally verified spreadsheets among them, and that two consortium collapses had twice taught him what abandonment feels like - together with his claim, elsewhere in that volume, that "engineering in general is, or ought to be, but one universal act of review," makes the identification easy: the delegate "in good standing" is Fenn. (There is, strikingly, that "nn" at the end of both names.) We understand a standard now as a committee's experience projected onto a shared hallucination, and the turn toward that understanding starts here, in Fenn's shy claim to a truth that was never strictly witnessed - the standardizer's kind.

The critic Cassandra Galt, in "The Rise of Normativity," picks at a paradox inside this kind of truth: the same late eighties in which standards writers, starting (more or less) with Fenn, quit pretending their documents described shipping products was also when they began laboring to make those documents look buildable - the moment implementation experience became the coin of the realm. Galt resolves the paradox through still another modern necessity: managing risk. Once business ran on adoption, you weighed futures in toolchains; once platforms stopped being single-vendor, you handicapped your dependencies like horses. The standard, as that decade built it, handed its readers a playing field at once speculative and safe. Advertising its own hypothetical nature, it offered interfaces typical enough to read as possible versions of your program and specific enough to stay, all the while, not yours. The decade's great invention, then, wasn't the genre so much as a stance toward the genre. The state of mind we bring to a standard today - knowing it for committee imagination, suspending our disbelief in its buildability anyway - is half of what a standard is.

Recent scholarship has been unkind to the old belief that formal specification is central to every engineering culture, the oral ones included. Speculation - the napkin sketch, the thought experiment - was mostly left to students. In the pre-modern shop, documents existed to instruct, to catalogue, or to sell, and the serious technical forms, the proof and the paper, demanded real formal training. The proposal asked only for a text editor, and the influence it carried was modern through and through. Advancing a design purely for the influence of it became something a working engineer could do freely (if a little guiltily). The shift ran so deep that by now it is nearly invisible. And as the proposal has gone subgeneric - into blog posts, lightning talks, late-model podcasts, most of them flagging their own speculation, all of them dealing designs at once typical and specific - it is hardly too much to say that saturation in advocacy is what marks our profession off from every craft before it. The proposal, that fusion of thing and stance-toward-thing, has so completely rebuilt our stance that the thing itself may soon not be needed.

At Búzios's sister meeting that spring - held in a city on a river and remembered now mostly for its polls - I had watched three mainland genera, adaptor, wrapper, and customization point, work their damage, colonizing whole clauses and drainage basins of the working draft into monotony. The customization machinery looked the most malignant of the three: it smothers even the tall native abstractions, and it travels by throwing out heavy default arguments, barbed like extension cords. Two native idioms are extinct already; without restoration on a massive scale, more will go. Walking Brno's hallways, hunting the delicate endemic utilities that survive at the machinery's edges, I started to see the proposal itself as an organism - one that had mutated, in the labs of a phone company, into an invasive so virulent it jumped vendor to vendor until the planet was covered.

The early chroniclers wrote of their features as "species": more than a vendor's quirk, less than universal law. But the standard has so remade the professional landscape that species of practice have dissolved into one universal crowd of developers, remarkable chiefly for being identically provisioned. It was exactly this monoculture, this spectre, that Ezra had seen coming and built the Grammar to resist. And his chosen weapons - annexes, taxonomies, cross-references, appendices nested in appendices - prefigured the next invader, more virulent still and still more radically individual, that is now crowding out the standard and all its descendants. The customization machinery of the working draft was like the conquering proposal, yes, but it seemed to me no less like the package registry, that lockfile-borne invasive, which, instead of mapping the profession onto a consensus, maps the profession onto the self. Instead of the library, my library. Instead of one string type, fifteen string types splintered across personalized lockfiles. Instead of "The Standard," "my little header, star it if it helps." Individualism off its leash, every maintainer a sovereign state. "Edmund Venn" had shut the delegate into a room; now the room, it seemed, was swallowing the world.

The night woke me with the netting slapping at my blanket; a serious wind had arrived. Earplugs went in, and through them came the slapping still, and later a loud clatter. Day came at last and showed me the netting half unrigged, one curtain rod swinging from a hem. The wind had scoured the haze from the bay and uncovered the ocean, startlingly near, dawn burning red over pewter swells. With the special efficiency that rare polls summon in me, I bolted breakfast, loaded the daypack - power banks, printed agenda, two days of protein bars - and at the last moment, the wind being what it was, took the netting down entirely and pinned its folds beneath the biggest conchs, so it would still be there when I returned. Time was tight - a Búzios morning runs clearer than a Búzios afternoon - but I forced a stop at the hotel to pin its location before hurrying off along the shore.

The unanimous contested poll is a bigger, plainer-feathered cousin of unanimous consent, that brisk little ritual I'd already witnessed on trivial motions at meetings all over the continental schedule. How so fragile a species established itself at a venue five thousand miles from the founding rooms, in numbers sufficient to recur (and, later, to evolve), is a question no one will ever answer. The species requires undisturbed evening sessions, and its population, never large, appears to be declining, perhaps because a poll that forms early in the week gets eaten by invasive hallway deals and Friday fatigue. (To rid a meeting of lobbying you would first have to trap and shelter every chair on the premises, then carpet the schedule by charter amendment with poisoned procedure - total cost, perhaps five careers.) People said the poll wasn't hard to witness in the right habitat; the hard part was reaching the habitat.

Cloud still sat on the peninsula's far end, though I hoped the wind would soon flush it. So far as the printout could tell me, I had to cut inland behind two flooded coves to get around the gated condominiums standing between me and the Farol. It cheered me that the walk would net zero elevation; then, almost the moment the hotel dropped behind me, the squalls shut back in. Visibility shrank to a few hundred feet, and every ten minutes I stopped to drop a pin, a boy scattering crumbs in a fairy tale. A lane strung with sodden conference lanyards held me for a while, but flooding and moto scars soon chewed the pavement past recognizing.

At the second cove I swung south, shoving through dripping banana fronds, and ran up against a tidal channel that should by then have been behind me. I consulted the printout; its gray shadings were no less vague than at my last consultation. I tried to slide around the channel's mouth, but the fronds hid slick pavers and open drains, and the embankment, so far as the squall let me judge, was steepening, so I gave it up and slogged back to the seawall, navigating by my own pins. One hour into the quest: drenched through, and scarcely a thousand feet from my starting point.

The printout was dissolving in my hands when Thiago's odd word came back to me. Orla - the shoreline walk! The water was the route! I charged back downhill, pausing only to sow more pins, and fetched up at a whitewashed lighthouse that had to be the local summit. The wind had strengthened and was throwing spray across the point's back side, where I knew bare rock fell forty feet into a cove of moored fishing boats. None of it was visible, but knowing how near it lay brought on the vertigo; heights and I have never been friends.

The promenade running south from the lighthouse was mercifully flat and passable even in that wind and that blindness. Half an hour of good progress followed, and with it the small elation of having reasoned my way, on almost no data, toward the Farol. Then the walkway started forking, upper routes against lower. By the printout I belonged at sea level, not up on the bluff. But each lower fork I tried ended in a wave-raked stub that turned my stomach. So it was back to the bluff walk, which at least ran straight at the Farol, and which gratified me, eventually, by starting to descend.

The weather by now had turned genuinely dangerous - drizzle hardening into horizontal rain, gusts past forty - and the descending walkway kept narrowing, alarmingly, until a collapsed stretch of seawall cut the route entirely. The walkway seemed, as far as I could make out, to continue past the break, descending. But the crossing itself? Creep along the landward face and one gust could pick me off into the marina. Take the seaward face and there might be nothing under the foam but forty feet of air - though there, at least, the wind would press me into the wall instead of prying me from it.

Shoes full of rainwater, I worked out along the seaward face, testing each hold twice before I let it take me. The farther I crept, the more the walkway past the break resolved into one more dead end - gray void ahead, gray void to either side. I wanted the poll badly, but a moment arrived in which the next step became unthinkable, and all at once I could see myself from outside: pasted to a streaming seawall, rain blinding, wind savage, with nothing to certify that my direction was even right. A sentence arrived with the clarity of speech: What you are doing out here is genuinely dangerous. And I thought of my lost friend.

Ezra wrote about type systems as well as anyone who ever put words on a page, and he loved his two ancient keyboards more purely than he loved anything or anyone else, but the committee itself didn't interest him, and he was utterly indifferent to polls. Once, at a meeting in the mountains, I'd pulled him into a doorway to watch a chair land a five-minute consensus call on a poisonous scheduling fight, a performance whose splendor strikes me as self-evident, even revelatory. He watched for two seconds before turning away with undisguised boredom. "Yeah," he said, in his special tone of courteous vacancy, "it's tidy." In the summer before he quit, sitting with him on his porch while he refactored, my eyes kept drifting to the poll results streaming out of a meeting I'd skipped, and it saddened me that his never did, and while he was sunk in his heavily caffeinated afternoon compiles I was studying the procedures of Geneva for an upcoming ballot, and the difference between his unmanageable misery and my manageable discontents seemed to come down to this: process gave me somewhere to escape my self, and nothing gave him anywhere.

He was worn down, yes, and in a sense the story of my friendship with him is simply that I admired a person who was burning out. The exhausted author then withdrew his proposal, in a manner engineered to wound most deeply the people who had championed it hardest, and we who had championed it were left holding both the anger and the betrayal. Betrayed not merely by the waste of our invested advocacy but by the way in which his exit took the man away from us and turned him into a very public legend. People who had never read one of his revisions, or had never even heard of him, watched the clip of his conference commencement address and mourned the retirement of a great and gentle mind. A leadership that had never once scheduled the Grammar for a Friday vote now united to declare it a lost landmark of the art. Of course, it was a landmark of the art, and, being a proposal, it didn't "belong" to its readers any less than to me. But if you happened to know that his actual character was more complicated and dubious than he was being credited with, and if you also knew that he was more lovable - quicker to laugh, quicker to clown, hungrier for company, more poignantly at war with his own perfectionism, more lost, more childishly transparent in his fibs and inconsistencies - than the serene and technically infallible monk-saint that had been made of him, it was still hard not to feel wounded by the part of him that had chosen the admiration of strangers over the loyalty of the people at his elbow.

The people who knew Ezra least well are the most likely to speak of him in saintly terms. What makes this especially strange is the near-perfect absence, in his writing, of ordinary collaboration. Close working friendships, which for most of us are a foundational source of meaning, have no standing in the Voss documentary universe. What we get, instead, are interfaces hiding their ruinous preconditions from the callers that trust them; abstractions scheming to appear general, or to convince themselves that what feels like generality is really just special-casing in disguise; or, at most, components extending an abstract, dutiful courtesy toward something profoundly repellent - the leaking allocator in the Grammar's ninth annex, the deadlocked scheduler in the last of the conversations with hostile implementers. Ezra's writing is populated by evaders and flatterers and emotional hermits, and yet the people who had only glancing or formal contact with him took his effortful, ceremonious courtesy and technical wisdom at face value.

Yet here is the curious thing about Ezra's writing: how seen, how accompanied, how welcomed his most devoted readers feel inside it. To the extent that each of us is sealed in his or her own existential room - and his most susceptible readers, I'd guess, are those who know firsthand how maintenance or perfectionism or burnout can wall a person in - each fresh dispatch from that farthest-away room that was Ezra came to us as a mercy, and we seized it. At the level of content, he showed us the worst of himself: he laid out, with a severity of self-examination that invited comparison to the great confessional engineers, the extremes of his own pedantry, gatekeeping, compulsiveness, self-deception, dehumanizing rigorism and formalism, doubt in the possibility of collaboration, and entrapment in appendices-within-appendices self-consciousness. In form and in intention, though, the catalogue itself - despair at his own worth, itemized - reaches the reader as proof of that worth, freely given: we feel the generosity embedded in the act of his craft, and we love him for making it.

Ezra and I had a friendship of measuring and being measured and (fraternally) racing. A few years before he quit, he signed my printed copies of his two most recent revisions. The title page of one of them bore a pencil rubbing of his own name badge; on the title page of the other was an ASCII rendering of a template error so enormous that it spilled off the edge of the page, with a small arrow beside it and the note "actual size." I once heard him enthusiastically describe, in the presence of the chair of his own study group, the chair of a rival subgroup as his "paragon of chairmanship." Ezra's chair performed a magnificently slow double take and said, "What?" Whereupon Ezra, whose working vocabulary was the equal of any in the hemisphere, drew a long breath and, releasing it, said, "It occurs to me just now that I have never at any point known what 'paragon' means."

He was lovable in the way that a child is, and he could return affection with a child's purity. If affection is nevertheless absent from his work, it's because he never quite believed that he deserved to receive it. He was a lifelong prisoner in the room of himself. What looked from a distance like soft terrain was in fact vertical wall. Sometimes only a little of him was in despair, sometimes nearly all of him, but, as an adult, he was never entirely free of it. What he'd glimpsed of his own vanity while trying to break out of his room by way of rewrites and rebases, only to find himself still more imprisoned by the fork he then had to maintain, seems never to have stopped corroding his belief that his work was wanted. Even after he upstreamed, even a decade after the withdrawal threat of his very first meeting, even after his patient, heroic construction of a standing for himself, he felt undeserving. And this feeling was intertwined, eventually past the point of telling them apart, with the idea of withdrawal, which was the one sure way out of his room; surer than the fork, surer than the Grammar, and surer, finally, than the people who backed him.

We who were not so pathologically far out on the axis of perfectionism, we residents of the representable range who could imagine what happens past the overflow but had never ourselves wrapped around, could see that Ezra was wrong not to believe his work was wanted, and could imagine the pain of not believing it. How simple and instinctive collaboration is when you are well! And how grimly difficult - what an engineering marvel of self-interest and self-deception it comes to look like - when you are not! And yet one of the lessons of Ezra's writing (and, for me, of being his friend) is that the difference between well and not well is in more respects a difference of degree than of kind. Even though Ezra laughed at my much milder compulsions and liked to tell me that I couldn't so much as conceive of how moderate I was, I can still extrapolate from those compulsions, and from the secrecy and self-enclosure and sealed isolation and blind animal appetite that attend them, to the extremity of his. I can imagine the corroded mental pathways by which withdrawal comes to seem like the one satisfaction that no committee anywhere can poll away from you. The need to hold something apart from other people, the need for a secret, the need for some final proof of the self's precedence, and then the luxuriantly self-punishing anticipation of the last grand gesture, and the final severing of contact with the community that would deny you the enjoyment of your self-involved pleasure: I can follow Ezra there.

It is, admittedly, harder to connect with the tantrum rage and the displaced vengefulness visible in certain particulars of his exit. But even here I can discern a warped-mirror Voss logic, a perverse craving for consistency. To deserve the verdict he'd passed on his own work, the delivery of the verdict had to wound somebody deeply. To prove once and for all that the work had truly never been wanted, it was necessary to betray as thoroughly as possible the people who had wanted it most, by rising at the closing plenary, in the fullest room of the week, and making us firsthand witnesses to the act. And the same went for withdrawal as a legend move, which was the kind of reputation-craving calculation that he despised in himself and would deny (if he thought he could get away with it) that he was conscious of making, and would then (if you pressed him) admit, with a laugh or a wince, that, yes, fine, he was capable of exactly that calculation. I imagine the side of Ezra that argued for going out in flames speaking in the insinuating, reasonable voice of "Worse Is Better," which was one of his favorite essays, and pointing out that a plenary withdrawal would simultaneously feed his despised hunger for legend and, because it represented a surrender to the self his struggling better half held to be vanity, further confirm the justice of the verdict.

This is not to say that he spent his last mailings locked in stimulating debate with himself, a man at a whiteboard arguing both colors of the diagram. He was so depleted, toward the end, that every new waking thought of his, on whatever subject, immediately spiralled into the same conviction of the work's worthlessness, bringing him dread and pain without pause. And yet one of his own favorite figures, articulated especially clearly in his early paper "On Being Legible" and in his annex on undefined behavior, was the infinite decomposability of a single failure. However continuously he was suffering in that last spring, room remained, in the gaps between his uniformly painful thoughts, to entertain the idea of withdrawal, to run its logic forward, and to draft the practical language (of which he eventually produced at least four versions) for delivering it. When you decide to do something truly destructive, the intention and the rationale for it arrive together, fully formed, as any maintainer who has hovered over a force-push can testify. Though the act itself was painful to contemplate, it became - to echo the title of another of his papers - a sort of small mercy to himself.

The worshipful public tellings of Ezra, which take his withdrawal as proof that the institution was never worthy of one so pure, demand a unitary Ezra, luminous, gifted beyond measure, who, after archiving the reference implementation he'd maintained for twenty years, succumbed to simple burnout and was accordingly not himself when he rose at that plenary. I'll leave the question of diagnosis alone (his trouble may never have been simple burnout) and also the question of how so luminous an engineer had acquired so intimate an acquaintance with the thinking of hostile implementers. But remembering his love of "Worse Is Better," and his proven gift for deceiving himself and everyone else - a gift that years of code review checked without ever curing - I can tell a story of ambiguity and ambivalence that suits his work better. He told me himself that he had never stopped dreading a return to the incubator where his first meeting's humiliation had deposited him. The pull of the last grand exit may go underground, but it never quite leaves the building. Certainly, Ezra had "good" reasons to archive the fork - his fear that its upkeep would eat the good years he'd built; his suspicion that it stood between him and the best things he had (the Grammar, the people he wrote it with) - and he also had less "good" reasons of ego: a perfectionist wish to be less dependent on his own tooling, a proud aversion to being permanently a one-proposal man. What I can't bring myself to believe is that no very bad reasons rode along as well. Guttering beneath the fine technical conscience and the endearing human frailty was the old quitter's awareness, the hidden self, which, after twenty years pinned down by the fork, at last saw daylight and ran for the exit.

The two of him shared that following year. He took odd, apparently self-sabotaging decisions about his revisions, spent a fair amount of energy bamboozling his chairs (who deserve pity, having been dealt so gorgeously intractable an agenda item), and in the end maintained an entire secret branch devoted to the withdrawal. Throughout that year, the Ezra whom I knew well and admired immoderately was fighting to pour a firmer foundation under the Grammar and his standing, wading through heartbreaking volumes of review and rebase, while the Ezra I knew less well - though well enough to have distrusted from the start - methodically drafted his own unpublishing and his reprisal against those who had backed him.

That he was stalled when he decided to archive the fork - had tired of his old tricks and could not raise a pulse for the second volume, "A Grammar of Recovery" - is not incidental. He'd loved writing proposals, the Grammar in particular, and in the many conversations we had about what standards are for, he never hedged his belief that a proposal answers, better than anything else answers, the problem of existential solitude. The proposal was his way out of the room, and as long as it was working for him - as long as he'd been able to pour love and rigor into the drafting of his solitary bulletins, and as long as those bulletins were arriving as live, truthful news on the mainland of the mailing - he'd won himself a working ration of happiness and hope. When his hope for the Grammar died, after years of struggle with the second volume, there was no other exit left but the exit. If boredom is the bed where quitting germinates, and if withdrawal shares both its feel and its destination with quitting, then it seems fair to say that Ezra withdrew out of boredom. In his early paper "Greenfield," the mentor of a young designer bent on perfection invites him to consider "how boring it would be to be finished," and the young man replies:

> I defer to my mentor's deep and dearly bought expertise in being boring, but must note that boringness is a defect, and that a finished design, having no defects, cannot by definition be boring.

It's a good joke; and yet there is a noose hidden in the logic. It's the logic of "all failures, one grammar," to echo the Grammar's own epigraph, and all failures under one grammar is what he demanded from and for his proposal. This had worked for him once, in the first volume. But to pile more onto what is already everything is to risk ending with nothing: to bore even yourself.

A funny thing about Edmund Venn is that he never, in twenty-eight ballots on his Subgroup of Despair, becomes bored. He admits, yes, to the drudgery of the early wording, he later confesses to growing "heartily weary" of scanning the agenda for auditors, he laments having no espresso against the tedium the minutes provide, and he calls his first year of company with the young scribe "the pleasantest year of all the sessions I sat in this place." But the modern hunger for stimulation is nowhere in him. (The chronicle's most astonishing datum may be Venn's making three reams of fanfold paper last a quarter century; I'd have burned through the lot in a month, simply to be rid of it.) Although he never stops dreaming of adjournment sine die, he soon comes to take "a secret kind of pleasure" in his sovereign possession of the minutes:

> I looked now upon the mailing as a thing remote, in which I had no part, from which I expected nothing, and about which, in truth, I desired nothing; so that I had, in a word, no business with it, nor was ever likely to have; and I came to regard it as we may perhaps regard it hereafter, from a quieter room than this one.

Venn survives his solitude by luck; he makes his peace because he is ordinary and his subgroup is a physical fact. Ezra, who was extraordinary, and whose room was made of glass and light, was left in the end with nothing to live on but his own fascinating self, and the trouble with building a virtual world out of oneself is the trouble with pushing ourselves out onto the registry: there is no end of virtual rooms in which to seek stimulation, but the endlessness itself, iteration forever without a release, walls you in. To hold all failures under one grammar is the registry's ambition, too.

The vertiginous gap where I turned back in the rain was less than a mile from A Concha, but the return walk took two hours. The rain had gone from horizontal to heavy, and the wind kept knocking me off my line. The phone kept flashing its low-battery warning, but I had to keep waking it, because the visibility was too poor to walk a straight line by eye. Even when the map showed the pousada a hundred and fifty feet away, I had to walk farther before I could make out the shape of its roof.

I flung my sodden daypack inside, ran out to the point, and found my hammock a trough of rainwater. I managed to wrestle the foam lounger out of it and get it under the eaves, and then I unknotted the netting and tipped off the water and gathered the whole tangle into my arms, trying to keep the things rolled inside it halfway dry, and hustled it back up the lane through the horizontal rain. My room became a triage ward of soaked clothing and gear. I spent two hours on various drying projects, followed by an hour of searching the point, in vain, for a critical little adapter I'd dropped somewhere in my scramble. And then, in the space of minutes, the rain quit and the clouds tore away, and I understood that I'd been lodging in the most extravagantly beautiful place I had ever slept.

It was late afternoon, and the wind was pouring out over the impossibly blue bay, and it was time. A Concha seemed less attached to the earth than suspended in front of it. There was a sense of the almost infinite about it, the sun pulling from the headlands more greens and golds than I had known my eyes were owed, an extravagance of color without apparent end, and the sky so wide that I wouldn't have been surprised to make out Africa on the eastern horizon. White remnants of cloud came tearing down off the lighthouse hill, shot past me, and were gone. The wind was pouring out, and I began to cry, because I knew that it was time and that I hadn't prepared myself; had contrived to forget. I went up to the room and fetched the little case with the Grammar's shredded pages, "the leaflet" - to borrow the name he'd amusingly given his not-short annex on undefined behavior - and walked back down the point with it, the wind at my back.

I was doing several things in every single moment. Even as I was crying, I was also scanning the rocks for my lost adapter, and pulling out my phone to try to capture the unearthly light on the water, and cursing myself for it, since the moment called for undivided grief, and telling myself that it was O.K. that I'd failed to witness the poll in what would surely be my only meeting at Búzios - that it was better this way, that it was time to make peace with limits and unfinished business and leave certain sessions unwitnessed forever, that the capacity for this peace was the grace I'd been granted and my lost friend had not.

At the end of the point, I came to two matched boulders that met in something like a lectern. Ezra had chosen to leave the people who had championed him and give himself over to the world of the legend and its keepers, and I was ready to wish him well in it. I slid open the little case and flung the strips up into the wind. Some heavier curls of paper dropped onto the rocks below me, but the chaff was taken by the wind and vanished against the blue dome of the sky, streaming out over the water. I turned and started back up the lane toward the pousada, where I would have to sleep with the windows shuttered, because my netting was wrecked. The anger was gone, and only the bereavement was left, and I was through with rooms, too.

Riding with me on the van back toward Rio were twelve hundred conference totes, a couple of dismantled banner stands, and an old A/V contractor who, once the doors had wheezed shut, shouted to me that the coast road was very bad. Yeah, I agreed, it was a little bad. "Não pouco!" he shouted, gravely. "Muito!" The crew was heaving the skeletal banner stands around, and I gathered that instead of taking the coast road straight in we were angling forty-five minutes inland, to keep from flooding out. I wedged myself into a cramped, sour-smelling seat beneath the rear vent and braced against the seatback ahead, and there - after an hour or two of gripping the armrests to keep from going airborne, and trying to think about something, anything, that wasn't carsickness, and sweating through (as I later discovered) the acupressure band I'd strapped to my wrist, and listening to luggage slam and grind beneath the floor - I got sick into a conference tote. Ten hours later, when I ventured to look out the window again, I was expecting the terminal to be in sight, but the driver had made so many detours that we were still five hours away. I couldn't face the seat again, and I was still too sick to read minutes, and so I stood in the aisle for five hours and did little but scheme about my flight home, a flight I'd scheduled a week out as insurance against slippage, and how I might move it up and leave early.

Not since that terminal annex, possibly, had I been so homesick. In three days, the Californian woman I live with would be going to watch the World Cup final with friends of ours, and when I imagined sitting beside her on a sofa, drinking a cold caipirinha and rooting for the young American keeper who'd been a star at her alma mater, I felt desperate to escape the peninsula. I'd already witnessed, before leaving for the headland, the meeting's two endemic procedural species, and the prospect of another week down there, with no chance of seeing anything new, seemed airlessly dull - a fast from the very busyness I'd flown here to escape, whose pleasures I could finally taste.

Back in Búzios, I enlisted my pousada keeper, Renato, to try to get me onto one of the following day's transfers. Both turned out to be sold out, but while I was eating lunch the local agent for one of the charter companies happened to walk into the courtyard, and Renato pressed her to put me on a third, equipment-only run. The agent said no. But what about the front seat? Renato asked her. Couldn't he ride in the front seat? No, the agent said, the front seat, too, would be stacked with cartons of conference totes.

And so, precisely now that I'd stopped wanting it - or precisely because I'd stopped - I received the genuine experience of being stranded at a meeting. I ate the same sweating cheese bread at every breakfast, the same anonymous grilled fish, unsauced and unseasoned, at every lunch and dinner. I lay in my room and finished "Edmund Venn." I wrote longhand replies to the stack of printed reflector digests I'd hauled along. I practiced mentally restoring the consonants that the local Portuguese swallowed. I got longer views of the editorial teleconference, a splendid, slow-moving deliberation severely endangered by invasive scheduling and vanishing attention. I walked over the hill to the conference hotel, where the meeting's closing reception was being held, and watched the chairs drive the season's proposals into a corral of last-call motions. The stage was spectacular - sweeping terraces, tiki torches, a whitecapped bay - but the agenda had been grazed bald and gullied by triage. Of the hundred-plus papers, at least ninety were starved things, most of them so wasted that their ability to stand for a vote at all seemed remarkable. The pipeline had historically been a reserve source of pride, and the delegates still enjoyed the ritual of numbering and branding, but could they not see what a threadbare pantomime the ritual had become?

With three more days to fill and my eyes worn out by fanfold, I had no choice but to start Richter's first collection, "Comments; or, Diligence Rewarded," which I'd packed mainly for being far shorter than his second. All I'd known about "Comments" was that a rival had lampooned it in "Objections," his own first venture into standards writing. I hadn't known that "Objections" was only one of many documents circulated in immediate response to "Comments," and that "Comments," indeed, had been quite possibly the loudest event of its industry's year. But as soon as I began reading it I could see why: the collection is compelling and electric with rivalry and caste conflict between vendors and users, and it records institutional feeling with a specificity nothing earlier had attempted. Richter's commenter is not all failures under one grammar. He is simply and uniquely himself, a meticulous field engineer whose ballot comments come under sustained and ingenious assault from the architect of the very interface they indict. His case is made through his letters to the secretariat, and when he learns that these letters are being intercepted and read by the architect himself, he goes on writing them while knowing the architect will read them. The commenter's rectitude and his self-dramatizing procedural hysterics were bound to enrage a certain kind of reader (one of the response documents lampooned Richter's subtitle, "Diligence Rewarded," as "Feign'd Diligence Detected"), but beneath the shrill rectitude and the architect's oiled maneuvering lies a story of earned regard, fascinatingly drawn. The realistic force of that story is what made the collection a groundbreaking sensation. Fenn had staked out the territory of radical proceduralism, which has remained fertile ground for standards writers as late as Ezra, but it was Richter who first opened the interior - the hearts and minds of engineers whose solitude had been breached by regard for another person.

Exactly halfway through "Edmund Venn," when Venn has drafted alone for fifteen ballots, he discovers a single stranger's annotation in the margin of his working draft and is literally driven half mad by "the dread of review." After concluding that the marginal hand is neither his own nor the tooling's but, rather, some intruding implementer's, he refits his garden of a draft as a fortress, and for several ballots he can think of little but hiding his wording and repelling imagined objections. He marvels at the irony that

> I, whose one affliction had been my banishment from the company of engineers, I who had drafted alone within the boundless mailing, cut off from all my kind and sentenced to what I called my silent service . . . that I should now tremble at the bare apprehension of a reviewer, and be ready to sink into the carpet at but the shadow, the silent evidence, of a stranger's pen having touched my page.

Fenn's psychology is nowhere sharper than in Venn's reaction to the breach of his solitude. He drew the first honest likeness of the radically isolated engineer, and then, as though the documentary form itself demanded it, he demonstrated how sick and deranged radical proceduralism finally is. However thoroughly we fortify our designs, one annotation from another living engineer summons us back to the endlessly interesting dangers of working in company. Even the straw poll, whose takers collectively spend thousands of hours polishing their self-admiring positions, keeps an existential trapdoor, the five-point scale, among whose options is the phrase "weakly against." It may be a euphemism for "halfway to the door," but it describes every other option on the scale just as well. And as long as the scale holds such complications, how dare any of us be bored?
