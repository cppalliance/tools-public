# Staker: Bitcoin Core

**A trillion-dollar public good whose consensus rules are set by a handful of unelected maintainers and underwritten by the industry that depends on them.**

Bitcoin Core is the unincorporated open-source project that maintains the reference implementation of the Bitcoin protocol and thereby defines de facto consensus for a network capitalized near $1.7 trillion.

July 2026, by Vinnie Falco


---

## 1. Executive Summary

**Key Judgments**

1. Bitcoin Core's contested consensus decisions are settled by a handful of merge-key holders who simultaneously present themselves as janitors with no unilateral control, a concentration of operational authority that the project's formal narrative is built to obscure rather than describe (medium-high).
2. Bitcoin Core's consensus rules are written almost entirely by developers paid by the industries those rules govern, and the money reaches them through a maintainer-selection pipeline concentrated in a single conduit, giving commercially interested funders durable structural leverage over who holds merge authority even though no funder has been shown dictating a merge (medium-high).
3. A multi-trillion-dollar network runs on a development budget on the order of $8.4 million a year, paid by a handful of funders while its wealthiest beneficiaries free-ride on a public good they cannot be charged for, leaving provision far below the level the aggregate stake would justify and the funding coalition thinner than the value it protects (high).
4. Bitcoin Core is losing its most capable maintainers to harassment and burnout faster than it is replacing them, and the surviving cohort has begun to treat those departures as the ordinary cost of the work rather than a warning (high).
5. Bitcoin Core's operational continuity rests on a single dominant merger who handles 56-65% of all merges and on undocumented tacit knowledge concentrated in a handful of contributors, with no named successor and no mechanism to appoint one (high).
6. The v30 relaxation of Bitcoin Core's OP_RETURN relay limit was settled over sustained objection by the holders of the merge keys, ratified as consensus, and in the process stranded roughly a quarter of nodes onto Bitcoin Knots. (medium)
7. Bitcoin Core's maintainers exercise governing authority over a base that never selected them and cannot remove them except by forking, and they sustain that authority on a legitimacy renewed by shipped code and Satoshi lineage rather than conferred by any mandate (medium).
8. At Bitcoin Core, serious consensus disputes have no binding channel of resolution: voice is thin, no low-cost arena can settle a substantive disagreement, and dissent can therefore be registered only by exit or fork, a lever now sharpened by maturing independent alternatives (medium).
9. Bitcoin Core depends entirely on discretionary third-party infrastructure - code hosting, a reproducible-build farm, continuous integration, and a developer mailing list - that each provider can withdraw at will, a dependency that has already failed once and that the state apparatus now prosecuting comparable software developers is positioned to weaponize into a kill switch (high).

Bitcoin Core is the reference implementation the wider ecosystem treats as the de facto consensus specification, and a network valued in the trillions depends on a development effort on the order of $8.4 million a year that only a handful of industry funders sustain. The defining backdrop to every finding below is that disproportion: a public good of extraordinary scale secured by a subsidy a single mid-size firm would not notice.

The single most important finding is concentrated control that disclaims itself. A merge-key inner circle of roughly five to six people settles contested consensus outcomes by controlling when and whether a change is merged, while formally denying that it governs at all - the dynamic developed in The Janitor's Grip. Because the same disclaimer that describes the power also insulates it, concentration and unaccountability arise from one mechanism rather than two, and the result is an accountability sink in which responsibility is so diffused that no electorate, board, or binding arena can pin it. The spine is stable to intensifying: a single maintainer's share of merges has risen to roughly 65 percent, and the lead-maintainer seat has stood deliberately vacant since 2023 (medium-high).

Who benefits diverges sharply from who is stated to benefit. The stated beneficiary is the broad public of Bitcoin users, holders, and independent node operators. In practice the primary beneficiaries are narrower: the incumbent maintainer in-group, which keeps concentrated and unaccountable control, and the commercial funder-complementors whose revenue rests on the base layer - exchanges, custodians, ETF issuers, layer-two firms, and data-embedding businesses - which underwrite nearly all the development that writes its rules, the channel traced in Funding the Hand That Merges. Core is thus nominally a commonweal project but substantially a business concern for those funders and a mutual-benefit association for its maintainers, leaving the diffuse user and holder base to bear consensus outcomes without a governance seat. The gap is structural rather than incidental: a permissionless, membership-free design never built intra-project accountability, so with no defined electorate and forking as the only ultimate check, concentrated operational power coexists with disclaimed responsibility (medium-high).

The trajectory splits in two directions. Functional and resource inputs are improving - Core completed its first external security audit, a $15 million Bitcoin Security Consortium was announced in July 2026, and the funder base is broadening. Procedural legitimacy, human capital, and voice are eroding in parallel: harassment has driven out several of the most capable maintainers, the v30 OP_RETURN concession moved roughly a quarter of nodes onto Bitcoin Knots, and dissent was suppressed during that change. The control spine holds or tightens while the surrounding legitimacy thins, so the net direction is a widening distance between concentrated, increasingly well-funded operational power and the accountability that would ordinarily bound it - and the same institutional money that broadens the resource base deepens the complementor alignment of the prime beneficiary (medium).

## 2. The Organization

Bitcoin Core is an unincorporated open-source project - not a legal entity - that maintains and releases the Bitcoin full-node client of the same name, supplying validation software, a wallet, and libraries such as libsecp256k1 ([bitcoincore.org](https://bitcoincore.org)). It descends directly from the original client Satoshi Nakamoto released in 2009. Development migrated to the bitcoin/bitcoin repository ([github.com](https://github.com)), created December 19, 2010, the day Gavin Andresen publicly assumed project management, and the parallel SourceForge SVN line closed with a final commit on September 13, 2011 marked "Development has moved to github" ([bitcoin-institute.pages.dev](https://bitcoin-institute.pages.dev)). The software carried the name "Bitcoin," under the copyright line "Bitcoin Developers," until the v0.9.0 release of March 2014 rebranded the client to "Bitcoin Core" to separate the software from the network. Work is organized as a GitHub "monotree" - the primary bitcoin/bitcoin repository, a separate bitcoin-core/gui repository whose master is mirrored in, and related projects such as libsecp256k1 - and as of mid-2026 the repository shows roughly 340 code contributors, 65 releases, and a latest release of v31.0 (April 2026), all under the MIT license. Being unincorporated, the project has no headquarters. Its contributors and funders are distributed internationally.

**Stated mission.** The project describes itself as "an open source project which maintains and releases Bitcoin client software called 'Bitcoin Core'" (bitcoincore.org). Its 2016 vision statement frames the goal as expanding Bitcoin "to work efficiently at extremely high scale, while at the same time maintaining security and the core properties of decentralization" and building "the foundation for additional layers," while stressing that "the Bitcoin Core development team does not decide the Bitcoin consensus rules" and that users choose which software to run ([bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org)).

Governance is off-chain and meritocratic, with no formal voting. The project distinguishes maintainers, who hold commit access and merge patches, from contributors, who propose, review, test, translate, or document. The maintainer role is held "by agreement of project contributors," and maintainers are selected by existing contributors (bitcoincore.org; [frontiersin.org](https://frontiersin.org)). Anyone may open or review a pull request, and a maintainer merges a patch on a judgment of fit with project principles, minimum inclusion standards, and contributor consensus read from public ACK/NACK signals across GitHub, the development mailing list, and IRC (github.com; frontiersin.org). The lead-maintainer line ran from Gavin Andresen (2010) to Wladimir van der Laan (laanwj), who took it on April 8, 2014. Van der Laan stepped back in August 2022 citing burnout and a wish to further decentralize governance, then removed his own merge privileges in February 2023, leaving the lead-maintainer role vacant since ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org); bitcoin-institute.pages.dev; [coingeek.com](https://coingeek.com)). Merge access now sits with a small group: one secondary source lists six maintainers as of 2026 - Michael Ford (fanquake), Ava Chow (achow101), Gloria Zhao (glozow), ryanofsky, hebasto, and TheCharlatan - while both Pieter Wuille (sipa) and van der Laan stepped down as maintainers around the August 2022 transition ([spark.money](https://spark.money); github.com). Maintainers cannot force protocol changes: node operators must voluntarily download and run releases, and the ultimate check is the ability to fork the code (spark.money; bitcoin.org).

**Funding is externalized.** The project itself holds no treasury and pays no one. Its developers are volunteers or are salaried through grants, fellowships, or employment from a small set of nonprofits and companies ([ccn.com](https://ccn.com); [bitcoinmagazine.com](https://bitcoinmagazine.com)). As of 2026 the principal full-time conduits are the nonprofits Brink, OpenSats, the Human Rights Foundation, and the MIT Digital Currency Initiative, and the companies Chaincode Labs, Blockstream, and Spiral (a Block, Inc. unit). The historical predecessor conduit was the now-defunct Bitcoin Foundation, formed in 2012 (ccn.com; [brink.dev](https://brink.dev); spark.money).

Under the "cui bono" typology of (Blau and Scott 1962), Bitcoin Core is best read as a commonweal organization: its output is a free public good whose prime beneficiary is the public at large - any user or node operator on the network - rather than dues-paying members, owners, or a defined client set. The software is MIT-licensed and free to run, there is no ownership stake or membership tier, and the project states plainly that "anyone can contribute" (github.com; bitcoincore.org). The reading applies to the productive core rather than a legal entity: Bitcoin Core has no legal personality, and the adjacent entities that fund it fall into other classes - business concerns such as Blockstream and Chaincode Labs, and charities such as Brink and OpenSats - so classifying the code project as commonweal is a judgment on its output rather than a legal characterization. (medium)

### Scope

- **Decision served:** this assessment maps where effective control over Bitcoin Core resides, who depends on it, and who pays for it, to judge how durable the current arrangement is and where it is most exposed.
- **Key assumptions:** the productive code project is analytically separable from the adjacent entities that fund it and from the broader Bitcoin network. Third-party crawler and disclosure figures current to mid-2026 are treated as ranges rather than precise counts. The commonweal reading applies to the code project, which has no legal personality, rather than to any incorporated entity.
- **Out of scope:** the merits of specific consensus-rule changes, Bitcoin's price and macroeconomics, the internal operations of adjacent funders beyond their role as development conduits, and any legal characterization of the unincorporated project.

## 3. The Landscape

### Market position

Bitcoin Core is the reference implementation of the Bitcoin protocol and defines de facto network consensus, so alternative implementations must match its behavior to remain on the network ([d-central.tech](https://d-central.tech); spark.money). It is the dominant node software by a wide margin: independent crawlers place it at roughly 75% to 78% of reachable nodes, with [bitdis.org](https://bitdis.org) measuring 75.48% (17,831 of 23,623 nodes), [coin.dance](https://coin.dance) showing 18,374 of 23,795 (about 77%), and Spark and Casa/Jameson Lopp citing about 77% to 78% (spark.money; [theblock.co](https://theblock.co)). Core's validation engine is now packaged as a standalone library, libbitcoinkernel, that other projects can embed (d-central.tech).

The only meaningful rival is Bitcoin Knots, a C++/MIT fork of Core maintained by longtime contributor Luke Dashjr with stricter default mempool and relay policies, at roughly 22% to 24% of reachable nodes (bitdis.org 24.27%, 5,733 nodes; coin.dance 22.7%, 5,390 nodes; spark.money about 22.7%). Beyond Knots the field is marginal: btcd (Go, ISC, about 0.05% to 0.08%, a clean-room reimplementation often used as a Lightning/LND backend), libbitcoin (C++, AGPL, under 0.01%), bcoin (JavaScript, MIT, dormant since v2.2.0 in November 2021), Gocoin, and the Utreexo-based lightweight nodes Floresta (Rust) and utreexod. Crawler tails also list Libre Relay (about 0.11%), Bitcore, Bitcoin Unlimited, Bitcoin UASF, Statoshi, Bitcoin Classic, and TRB, all at or near zero (bitdis.org; coin.dance; spark.money; d-central.tech).

The reachable node population is rising: Bitnodes recorded a 19% gain during 2024 to 20,367, and mid-2026 snapshots show roughly 24,500 to 25,000 ([btcnodes.io](https://btcnodes.io) 24,600 to 25,252; spark.money about 24,500), a reported 14% gain across 2025 ([blog.lopp.net](https://blog.lopp.net); btcnodes.io). Counting unreachable nodes, Lopp cited a 49% rise from about 65,000 to 96,852 during 2024, and Spark estimates 50,000 to 100,000 in total (blog.lopp.net; spark.money). The operator base is geographically concentrated in the United States (about 32%, 2,754 nodes), Germany (about 17%, 1,469), France (about 7%), Canada (about 5%), the Netherlands (about 4%), Finland (about 4%), and the United Kingdom (about 4%) (btcnodes.io). The workforce reversed a multi-year decline to 135 unique code contributors in 2025 (up from about 112 in 2024, after a roughly 193 peak in 2018). That count stands against a frequently cited "core developer" set of about 41, five to six maintainers holding merge authority, and more than 1,000 all-time contributors since 2010, with mailing-list traffic up 60% year over year and about 285,000 lines changed (theblock.co; [theblockbeats.info](https://theblockbeats.info); [odaily.news](https://odaily.news); [christinedkim.substack.com](https://christinedkim.substack.com)). At network scale the software secures a hashrate of about 1,000 EH/s (up tenfold since 2020), a UTXO set of about 173 million (about 11 GB, tripled since 2018), a blockchain exceeding 600 GB and growing about 17% a year, Lightning capacity near 5,000 BTC, and a Bitcoin market capitalization near $1.7 trillion (spark.money; theblockbeats.info).

The ultimate downstream user base - Bitcoin owners, distinct from the node-operator constituency - is estimated only loosely. Counts range from about 106 million on conservative on-chain and exchange counts (Bitbo/Chainalysis), through about 365 million on Crypto.com's blended 2025 figure, to about 467 to 500 million including indirect ETF and custodial exposure (CoinShares/Glassnode), roughly 1.3% to 6% of world population ([bitbo.io](https://bitbo.io); [rothbard.eu](https://rothbard.eu); [bleap.finance](https://bleap.finance); [solcard.cc](https://solcard.cc); [oobit.com](https://oobit.com)). (low-medium)

The closest structural peer is the client ecosystem of other major public blockchains, principally Ethereum, which deliberately maintains multiple independently developed execution and consensus clients and sets an explicit target that no client exceed 33% share, warning that a client above a two-thirds supermajority could finalize a buggy chain ([ethereum.org](https://ethereum.org); [clientdiversity.org](https://clientdiversity.org); [execution-diversity.info](https://execution-diversity.info)). Ethereum execution-client shares in 2025 to 2026 ran roughly Geth about 50% (clientdiversity.org) or 41% (Lido scorecard via [ainvest.com](https://ainvest.com)), Nethermind about 25% to 43%, Besu about 9% to 16%, Reth about 7%, and Erigon about 6% (clientdiversity.org; ainvest.com; ethereum.org). Benchmarked against that norm, Bitcoin's implementation field is far more concentrated - a single reference implementation near 77% plus one dominant fork, with no analogous sub-33% diversity target - a contrast commentators frame as simplicity and protocol stability traded against implementation-bug resilience (spark.money; ethereum.org). (medium-high)

### Ecosystem dependencies

Core's cryptographic foundation is libsecp256k1, a separate high-assurance C library with no runtime dependencies that performs ECDSA and Schnorr signing and verification. It replaced OpenSSL in Bitcoin Core v0.12 in early 2016 after OpenSSL signature-parsing inconsistencies risked chain splits, and delivered a 2.5x to 5.5x verification speedup (github.com; bitcoinmagazine.com; blog.blockstream.com). Development is hosted entirely on a single platform, GitHub, at bitcoin/bitcoin (github.com). Because Core holds no treasury, the project depends on external funders to salary its developers - Brink, Chaincode Labs, the MIT Digital Currency Initiative, Spiral (Block), and Blockstream among them (theblock.co; christinedkim.substack.com; theblockbeats.info; [bitmex.com](https://bitmex.com)). Downstream, the Lightning Network and Liquid depend on libsecp256k1 and its libsecp256k1-zkp variant, and miners, exchanges, payment processors, and Layer 2 operators depend on Core's settlement validation being correct (blog.blockstream.com; spark.money; github.com).

### Domain-specific vulnerabilities

An OP_RETURN policy schism now splits the network: Core v30 and later raised the OP_RETURN limit to 100 KB and allow multiple outputs, and crawlers show about 26.89% of nodes on the new policy against 73.11% on the legacy 83-byte limit, alongside a BIP-110 soft-fork signaling effort to restrict arbitrary data (bitdis.org). The dispute drove a sharp migration to Bitcoin Knots: from 69 nodes in January 2024 to about 4,200 (18%) by September 2025, peaking near 25%, then 5,599 (22.6%) by February 2026 - roughly an 8,000% increase in fourteen months attributed to the OP_RETURN/datacarriersize controversy and Core v30's removal of data limits rather than to technical merit. Knots itself remains at v29.3 and has not integrated the v30 or v31 codebase, so it gains nodes while falling behind technically ([hypersinc.com](https://hypersinc.com); [studyknots.github.io](https://studyknots.github.io); spark.money).

Two developments cut the other way. In November 2025 Core completed its first public third-party security audit, conducted by Quarkslab, funded by Brink, and focused on the peer-to-peer networking layer, finding no critical or high-severity vulnerabilities (theblock.co). On the funding side, VanEck has pledged 5% of its spot Bitcoin ETF profits to Brink, tying an external commercial revenue stream to Core development (theblock.co).

## 4. The Janitor's Grip: a handful of merge-key holders settle contested consensus while disclaiming that they govern at all

Bitcoin Core's contested consensus decisions are settled by a handful of merge-key holders who simultaneously present themselves as janitors with no unilateral control, a concentration of operational authority that the project's formal narrative is built to obscure rather than describe (medium-high). The self-image and the structure point in opposite directions: contested outcomes trace to a few maintainers, and by merge share to nearly one, while the surrounding language attributes every decision to "rough consensus" and "the community." The same disclaimer that describes the power also insulates it, so concentration and unaccountability are produced by one mechanism rather than two.

**The instrument is the merge.** Whoever holds the merge on a contested change decides which "consensus" is recognized and when, so merge timing is the operative form of control rather than any formal vote (Dahl 1957). In 2025 a single maintainer, Michael Ford (fanquake), merged between 56 percent by Brink's own funding report and roughly 65 percent by a reform-side analysis of all changes, against a base of about 135 contributors. Concentrated component ownership is normal for the peer class, and the Ethereum client geth also concentrates ownership through CODEOWNERS, but a single-person merge share in the 56 to 65 percent range is a specific deviation beyond that baseline, not an instance of it. The clearest demonstration is the v30 OP_RETURN sequence, where Gloria Zhao (glozow) merged the contested change (PR #32406) and Ava Chow (achow101) later merged the concession (PR #33453): the outcome moved with whoever held the merge, and the substance of that fight is treated in The v30 Concession. A competing reading holds that high merge throughput is partly an ordinary willingness to do unglamorous volume, which is true but explains the volume only, leaving the control of contested consensus by merge timing unexplained (medium-high).

The maintainer role has been converted rather than designed (Mahoney and Thelen 2010). The nominally janitorial maintainer function now carries de facto agenda-setting and gatekeeping power, visible in Ford's merge-order influence and Chow's decisive concession timing, and the funding conduits meant to preserve developer independence have become the maintainer-selection pipeline, with Brink training running upstream of maintainership. Role ambiguity of this kind is the informal-governance baseline, and maintainers still execute community-reviewed work on routine changes. What exceeds the baseline is the documented redeployment of the role in contested cases and the funding-to-selection channel that Funding the Hand That Merges develops (medium-high).

**Concentration is self-perpetuating.** A distributed-governance self-image coexists with the top three maintainers controlling 81.1 percent of all-time merges, a contributor Gini of 0.851, top-ten pull-request control rising from 42.7 to 49.8 percent, and a succession in which challengers never displace incumbents, which is the iron law of oligarchy in operation (Michels 1911). Authority rests on an informal, co-opted inner circle with no publicly documented rule set, selection that is "neither formalized nor transparent," power "kept deliberately opaque," and full measured voting cohesion between the two lead figures. Because that authority was never formally granted, it cannot be formally revoked (Freeman 1972). The benign account, that a minority doing most of the work earns proportional influence with forking as the ultimate check, cannot explain either the deliberate opacity or the absence of any revocation mechanism, and forking is exit rather than intra-project accountability. Selection also appears to be settled before it is ratified (Helmke and Levitsky 2004): the one documented nomination (TheCharlatan) carried "the approval of the four other core maintainers before publicly proposing" him, so the public ACK-weighted process ratified a decision already made. That last strand rests largely on a single case, and pre-socializing a new key holder is defensible vetting rather than a bypass, so it is carried here at moderate weight while the concentration and closed-succession findings behind it are strong.

**The narrative is decoupled from the operation.** The formal story of a decentralized, janitorial project with "no unilateral control" and disclaimed authority is disconnected from concentrated merge control and decisive timing on contested changes, and it functions substantially to satisfy external audiences such as courts, users, and regulators while work proceeds by informal discretion (Meyer and Rowan 1977). The disclaimer is partly true and legally motivated, since maintainers genuinely cannot force node operators to adopt a release, but that covers only the adoption dimension and not the merge control and agenda-setting it is also used to deny. The residue is an accountability sink (Davies 2024): responsibility is diffused by design, maintainers "deliberately disclaim responsibility and ownership over Bitcoin," decisions are attributed to "rough consensus" and "the community," and who holds the levers is "kept deliberately opaque," so no individual can be held to account for a consequential change. The genuinely diffuse-power reading again explains only adoption. It cannot explain the gap between concentrated merge control and disclaimed responsibility, which is the sink itself. The wider chain of unaccountable proxying that this disclaimer feeds is treated in Governing Without a Mandate.

Between the otherwise disconnected parties sit brokers (Burt 1992). BIP editors, historically Luke Dashjr alone, gate whether a proposal becomes a citable standard. Ford as merge coordinator is "often the first to comment" and controls merge order and conflict resolution. Brink is explicitly marketed as a "buffer" between corporate funders and developers. Single points of contact are expected at smaller scale, and the BIP-editor bottleneck was deliberately widened in 2024, but merge-order control and a marketed buffer are structural-hole positions that the small-scale explanation does not fit for a mature, high-stakes project. The janitorial gloss and the broker's real position are two descriptions of the same seat (medium-high).

Entrenchment has a benign twin. Altering the status quo requires assent across maintainers who merge, node operators who adopt, and miners who signal, an interest-distant multi-veto structure that entrenches incumbency, visible in the BIP-110 stall and the OP_RETURN schism, with ossification "treated as a desired end state" (Tsebelis 2002). The stronger reading of that same architecture is that multiple veto points are a deliberate stability design for consensus-critical software where an erroneous change is catastrophic, and the BIP-110 stall is that design working as intended. Tacit coordination among veto players to block change is not evidenced. The structure therefore holds mainly where it reinforces the closed succession rather than as independent proof of collusion. The related credible-commitment question resolves further toward the benign side (North and Weingast 1989). The 21 million cap and the consensus rules are protected by node veto and forkability, and OP_RETURN showed that commitment holding when nodes rejected the change through Knots. That rejection demonstrates that effective discretion over consensus rules rests with node operators rather than the merge chokepoint, even as that chokepoint remains a latent lever should node discipline ever lapse (low-medium).

Who benefits is legible from who holds the merge. The direct beneficiaries are the roughly six trusted-key holders, and disproportionately the single highest-share merger, whose position converts volume into agenda control. Incumbents benefit again from a succession that no challenger has displaced. Those who pay are the roughly 135 contributors whose independent judgment is displaced when contested outcomes turn on merge timing, and the node operators and users who must accept a settled result narrated as consensus. The internal power relations are a tight center with a soft perimeter: strong cooperation and cohesion among the maintainers, a patronage relationship from incumbents to the new maintainers they pre-approve, and a cooperative tie from the merge coordinator to his predecessor (Ford and Wladimir van der Laan) that anchors continuity. The fault lines run outward from the center rather than through it, from proposal authors against the BIP gate in the Dashjr era to the ecosystem living downstream of editor decisions, which is consistent with a structure whose disputes are resolved by who holds the merge rather than by contest among equals (medium-high).

The collective of Bitcoin Core maintainers, about six trusted-key holders, is the definitive stakeholder of this assessment (Mitchell, Agle and Wood 1997): highest in power, legitimacy, and urgency, and the highest-agency actor in the system, deciding what ships and when. Formally they are janitors executing community consensus. In operation they are gatekeepers with discretion over merge order, timing, and which "consensus" is recognized, and that discretion is concentrated in fewer hands than the headcount implies once merge share is counted. The gap between stated and actual role is substantial, with the concentration documented and any external direction of it not. That unknown is a real gap, but the concentration holds regardless. Their trajectory is toward deeper concentration under a stable informal structure.

Michael Ford (fanquake) is the second definitive actor and, by merge share, the effective center of the dynamic. His formal role is day-to-day merge coordinator. His power base is the merge itself, amplified by being first to comment and by controlling merge order and conflict resolution, which makes him the de facto primary gatekeeper rather than one of six equals. What he stands to gain is continued agenda control. His latent exposure is that his Brink salary, now including ETF-issuer money, is an upstream influence channel with real structural leverage and no demonstrated direction, a dependency that Funding the Hand That Merges takes up, while his singular share is also the continuity risk examined in One Person Away From a Crisis. His trajectory is a deepening but so far unexercised chokepoint.

The BIP editors, now Mark "Murch" Erhardt and colleagues and formerly Luke Dashjr, are a dominant actor, holding real power and legitimacy over whether a proposal receives a number but low urgency. The seat is a bounded procedural chokepoint, deliberately narrowed in 2026 to on-topic and formatting checks, so it now confers less than it once could. Two latent signals persist: the editor bench overlaps commercial power centers, and the Dashjr era is the documented case of the gate used as a factional weapon, now paired with a fresh bus-factor as a single editor became effectively the sole active one in early 2026. Their trajectory is a weakened gate sitting atop an unresolved concentration.

The trajectory across these strands is intensifying rather than stabilizing: merge share and top-ten control are both degrading while the informal-selection machinery holds, so the pattern of concentrated power with disclaimed responsibility widens even as the individual entrenchment and commitment claims soften. The variable that would have to move first is perception, whether the roughly 135-contributor base and the wider node-operating public come to read the merge center as the real decision locus or continue to accept the no-leaders self-image, and nothing in the record shows it moving. Over the next two years, and absent an external shock that forces a formal governance change, the next contested consensus decision is likely to be settled again by whoever holds the merge rather than by any widening of independent judgment beyond the center, and to be narrated as consensus after the fact (medium). Whether actors outside the merge center have already stopped forming independent judgments, as opposed to merely deferring to save effort, is the question the same record cannot yet close (medium-high).

No intra-project mechanism currently exists to hold this concentration to account: selection is informal and undocumented, authority is non-revocable because it was never formally granted, and the only external check, forking, is exit rather than accountability. The specific absent mechanism, scoped to what a roughly six-keyholder volunteer project could realistically adopt, is a written and revocable maintainer-authority rule, meaning published selection criteria, a named revocation path, and a lightweight guardrail on contested merges such as required co-merge or rotation on consensus-level changes. Nothing of this kind is in place, and the record names no existing mechanism to weigh for adequacy.

## 5. Funding the Hand That Merges: the industry that depends on Core's rules pays for nearly all the development that writes them

Bitcoin Core's consensus rules are written almost entirely by developers paid by the industries those rules govern, and the money reaches them through a maintainer-selection pipeline concentrated in a single conduit, giving commercially interested funders durable structural leverage over who holds merge authority even though no funder has been shown dictating a merge (medium-high). Nearly all full-time development is underwritten by a short list of sponsors - Brink, OpenSats, Chaincode Labs, Spiral, and Blockstream - and every sitting maintainer draws a salary from one of them. The leverage is exercised at the point of selection and continuation rather than at the point of merge: the claim is not that a sponsor ordered a change, but that the channel deciding who becomes and remains a maintainer runs through the institutions whose products depend on the base layer those maintainers guard. Where The Janitor's Grip profiles the handful of key-holders who settle contested changes, the question here is who pays them.

The dynamic is best read as institutional capture (Glaeser 2002) operating through selection, not instruction. Reporting indicates that "almost all newcomers to the Bitcoin Core maintenance team after 2022 have received Brink's funding or training," which routes the supply of future key-holders through one organization. With every current maintainer also employed by a sponsor, the result is a candidate pool shaped by commercial funders long before any merge decision is reached. The influence is latent and structural - it requires no intervention in a pull request, only the funding, training, and retention of the people who will handle them. The strongest benign reading, that practitioner funding of the code practitioners rely on is the ordinary condition of open-source infrastructure, does not dissolve the specific fact that resists it: the concentration of the newcomer pipeline in a single training-and-funding conduit (medium-high).

Around that pipeline sits the shape of capture rather than demonstrated capture. Core authors the de facto consensus rules, and the parties most exposed to them - exchanges, custodians, ETF issuers, and layer-two firms such as Blockstream and Block - fund nearly all full-time development through Brink, OpenSats, Chaincode, Spiral, and Blockstream. That the affected industry writes the rules it lives under is the classic form of regulatory capture (Stigler 1971), but the evidence supplies the shape without the act: no funder is shown dictating a merge, and practitioner involvement in drafting technical rules is also the normal route to competent oversight. The readings are not equal - the structural overlap of funder and rule-writer is documented while dictation is not - so this stands as a live structural risk, not a proven mechanism (low-medium).

The funding carries a consistent incentive. Sponsor support tracks the protection of revenue complements: Blockstream (Liquid, Lightning), Block and its Spiral unit (Cash App, LDK), and the exchanges and ETF issuers whose custody, trading, and assets under management all sit atop the base layer fund Core because, in the reporting's phrase, they "indirectly rely on" it. Their interests favor the base-layer conservatism that protects those complements - a complementor subsidy (Lerner and Tirole 2002). The benign reading is that corporate sponsorship of complementary open-source code is the ordinary FOSS baseline and that no divergence from the broad user interest has been shown in actual merges. The evidence establishes the incentive plainly but not its conversion into biased outcomes, which is why this is the incentive behind the capture thesis rather than its proof (medium).

The clearest confirmed finding is pervasive conflict of interest (Davis 1982). Dual roles are the norm rather than the exception: Luke Dashjr simultaneously maintains a competing fork, edits BIPs, and is Ocean Mining's CTO who filters transactions he opposes. Ava Chow (achow101) maintains Core while co-founding Localhost Research. Blockstream co-founders held merge access while running a company whose products depend on the base layer they helped shape. The benign gloss, that overlapping roles are unavoidable in a small specialized field where the hats are publicly known, does not reach the sharpest instance - a BIP editor actively filtering transactions tied to proposals he opposes. The conflict is structural and documented. What remains open is whether disclosure, where it exists, actually constrains the conflicted party.

Personnel move densely within the same small set. Careers cycle among the few funders and the maintainer group - Newbery from Chaincode to Brink co-founder, Gloria Zhao (glozow) from Brink to Chaincode, and Wladimir van der Laan (laanwj), Andresen, and Fields into the MIT Digital Currency Initiative - a revolving door (Kalmenovitz et al. 2023) that thickens the ties binding maintainers to sponsors. The benign reading holds that movement across a small employer set is unavoidable and does not by itself evidence a quid pro quo, and the specific influence mechanism, an anticipated future move shaping a present decision, is untested. The movement is well documented. Its causal effect on decisions is inferred, not observed (medium-high).

The gap these dynamics exploit is a principal-agent (Eisenhardt 1989) separation. Maintainers and developers decide protocol behavior while the diffuse base of users and holders bears the consequences under weak monitoring - a gap salient enough that Tulip Trading litigated the claim that developers owe users a fiduciary's "single minded loyalty." The countervailing fact is real: node operators monitor through the upgrade choice and retain the coarse checks of exit and fork, and the courts did not establish a fiduciary duty. The monitoring that exists is genuine but blunt, leaving the agency gap open without rendering it a breach (medium).

The defenses against all of this exist and partly hold. Participation is nominally individual rather than by employer, sponsors are plural, the code carries the permissive MIT license, Brink is explicitly marketed as a "buffer," and Spiral states that "Spiral decides... what we work on." These are functioning autonomy defenses (O'Mahony 2003), and no sponsor is shown attempting to steer direction. What strains them is concentration: when "almost all newcomers... after 2022 have received Brink's funding or training," the buffer itself pools into a single conduit, so the defense and the capture channel prove to be one fact seen from two sides (low-medium).

Beneficiaries and payers overlap. The funders, and the complement businesses behind them, benefit twice: they secure continued maintenance of infrastructure their products require, and they gain proximity to the people who decide what that infrastructure does. The diffuse user and holder base pays differently: it receives maintained software at no charge while ceding any structured say over who maintains it. The stated beneficiary of Core is the open network at large. The demonstrated beneficiary of the funding structure is the set of firms whose complements the base layer protects. That gap is not corruption but architecture - those who value Core most in dollar terms are those positioned to fund, and thereby select, its stewards. That so few funders carry a network its largest beneficiaries free-ride on is the subject of A Trillion-Dollar Good on an $8 Million Subsidy. The concern here is narrower - not the thinness of the base, but that those who fund also select (medium).

Internally, power follows dependency and patronage rather than command. The funders form a coalition of convenience: they rarely coordinate overtly, yet a shared interest in base-layer conservatism aligns them without a meeting. Brink is the broker - the one node through which both the newcomer pipeline and the most commercially fraught money, the ETF issuers, pass. Blockstream holds a different lever, a durable code dependency rather than a payroll. The fault line separates the conduit funders, whose leverage is selection, from the complement firms, whose leverage is the incentive their money carries. The July 2026 arrival of explicit ETF-issuer commitments to Brink is the first point at which that incentive turned overt on the funder side (medium).

**Brink** is the definitive stakeholder of this dynamic and its structural chokepoint. A grant-making nonprofit, its power base is reward (French and Raven 1959): it salaries the single-person bus factor among maintainers, Michael Ford (fanquake), and a second maintainer (hebasto), and it trains and funds the bulk of post-2022 newcomers. Marketed as a corporate "buffer," it recasts the origin of sponsor money as neutral grants, and the recent arrival of ETF-issuer capital (Strive, VanEck) inserts a commercial-incentive vector one step removed from visibility. Its agency over who maintains Core is high. Its agency over what merges is low - what the record shows is concentration rather than any direction of a merge (medium-high).

**Chaincode Labs** is a dominant funder whose power is likewise reward-based, exercised through employment and residencies that seed contributors into Core. Reporting calls it the "largest financial supporter," at roughly 46% of 2023 developer-employment spend, and its residency program is an agenda-setting pipeline into the maintainer pool. The clearest case of a contentious change originating inside the firm yet surfacing as an ordinary contributor proposal is a single well-documented instance, not a demonstrated pattern (medium).

**Spiral (Block, Inc.)** is formally an independent open-source unit and functionally a proxy for Block and Jack Dorsey's capital and strategy: its flagship LDK feeds Block's own Cash App, and it is one node in a broader Dorsey-linked funding web, having also helped seed the Dorsey-backed Ocean pool that deploys Knots. The parent linkage is high-confidence. Spiral's own statement that "Spiral decides... what we work on" is the autonomy claim set against it.

**OpenSats** holds the purse for a large share of independent contributors, a latent selection effect over who can afford to work on Core. Its governance - a nine-member board, recusal norms, high transparency - mitigates that channel more than any other funder's, so the exposure to watch is donor concentration (Tether, the Bitwise ETF, StarkWare) rather than the grant-making body itself (medium).

**Blockstream** exerts the most durable influence and the least payroll-based. Its power is expert and referent rather than reward: the code dependency on libsecp256k1, which every node runs and few can review deeply; a founder network embedded in Core's historical leadership; and Adam Back's agenda-setting voice. The library dependency is an under-appreciated, long-lived lever (medium-high).

**Jack Dorsey** is a hidden hand acting through intermediaries: capital flows through Spiral, Brink, OpenSats, Btrust, the Bitcoin Legal Defense Fund, and Ocean, while his formal role is nil. The distance between zero formal position and pervasive financial reach is the signal, tempered by the operational independence those intermediaries assert (medium-high).

**The MIT Digital Currency Initiative** is the strongest hidden-influence actor in this dynamic, holding power and legitimacy without urgency. It funds and hosts several load-bearing long-term maintainers, and its real leverage is selection power over the roster, exercised without command. The documented episode in which Jeffrey Epstein "gift funds" helped underwrite the initiative's launch as the "principal home and funding source" for Core developers, with Joi Ito resigning in 2019, shows the neutral-home framing has been compromised before - a live reputational-capture vector (medium-high).

**Maelstrom**, Arthur Hayes's family office, is a dominant but lower-intensity single-source funder tilted toward privacy work, writing $50,000-$150,000 twelve-month Bitcoin-denominated contracts through a two-person committee that connects directly into Brink's board via Jonathan Bier. A lone financier plus one well-connected node concentrates privacy-feature agenda influence beyond its modest dollar scale, with a pardoned-founder reputation-laundering angle (medium).

The remaining funders sit at low or contested salience and carry soft, single-vector influence. **Strive**, Matt Cole's asset manager, is the concrete mechanism behind the complementor concern: its July 2026 "Bitcoin Stewardship Commitment" funds Brink and BPI and, with VanEck, ties ETF profits to Brink - both the benign reading (normal sponsorship) and the pathological one (funding shaping the pipeline) survive. The **Human Rights Foundation's Bitcoin Development Fund** is a non-commercial funder ($11.8M in BTC across 367 projects) backing contributors for privacy and decentralization rather than return, overt and low-leverage. **ARK Invest** commits a share of management fees to open-source developers, a complementor that also funds. **Lightning Labs** is a major layer-two complementor maintaining LND on its own libraries, dependent on Core and a driver of implementation diversity. **Vinteum** builds a Latin American developer pipeline as a non-commercial host, the lowest-salience node here. Each adds an incentive vector. None is shown steering an outcome (low).

The trajectory is a static structure with a tightening channel: every maintainer stays sponsor-employed, while the complement-driven selection channel concentrates further in Brink as ETF money arrives. The selection concentration documented here is one half of a larger pattern completed in The v30 Concession, where a contested outcome is ratified under a consensus label. The open question is direction - whether the capture is being built by deliberate strategy or has settled in through gradual moral seduction, funders and maintainers each adjusting to a comfortable equilibrium no one chose. On present evidence the second reading is better supported, but the July 2026 consortium is the first overt instance of funder-side coordination and shifts the balance toward deliberateness at the margin.

If the July 2026 stewardship commitments set a pattern others follow, then within twenty-four months two or more commercially interested funders coordinate their support explicitly - jointly earmarking money to the same conduit or roster - turning today's tacit alignment of interest into overt coordination on the funder side. On the trend now visible, that outcome is likely. The forecast rests on a single documented instance generalized forward, which caps confidence (medium).

No remediation concern was routed to this dynamic, and none of the existing defenses is adequate to it. The mechanisms that would ordinarily contain sponsor influence - individual rather than corporate participation, a plurality of sponsors, the permissive license, and Brink's self-description as a buffer - are the ones concentration has hollowed, since buffer and capture channel have converged on one conduit. What is missing is a maintainer-selection process not dependent on a single funder-conduit. Within Core's current budget and unincorporated form the realistic step is not new spending but transparency - a standing, public disclosure of which sponsor funds or trained each maintainer and newcomer, so that the selection channel the base cannot vote on can at least be seen. Nothing in the present structure supplies it (medium).

## 6. A Trillion-Dollar Good on an $8 Million Subsidy: a few funders carry a network the largest beneficiaries free-ride on, and the coalition is one defection from a scare

A multi-trillion-dollar network runs on a development budget on the order of $8.4 million a year, paid by a handful of funders while its wealthiest beneficiaries free-ride on a public good they cannot be charged for, leaving provision far below the level the aggregate stake would justify and the funding coalition thinner than the value it protects (high). Everything else follows from that single disproportion: the dependency it creates for the payers, the fragility it leaves in the coalition, and the reputational exposure that would surface if the coalition were tested.

Bitcoin Core is a non-excludable public good. Every holder, exchange, custodian, ETF issuer, and miner consumes its output, and none can be billed for it. Classic collective-action theory (Olson 1965) predicts that such a large latent group under-provides, because no single member's stake, however large, is pivotal to whether the good exists. Each rationally waits for others to pay. That is the configuration here - a multi-trillion-dollar beneficiary base and a development budget on the order of $8.4 million a year, supplied by four to seven organizations. The gap between what the network is worth and what secures it is what matters. The rest is consequence.

The under-provision is not the ordinary open-source free-riding baseline. Downstream free-riding on permissively licensed software is universal, and a benign reading treats Core as one more MIT-licensed project whose users owe nothing. The magnitude defeats that reading: a network valued in the trillions is sustained by a sum a single mid-size firm would not notice, funded by a privileged subset while the largest wealth-holders on the network - exchanges, custodians, and ETF issuers holding millions of coins between them - contribute nothing and cannot be compelled to. The disproportion exceeds any ordinary free-riding baseline by orders of magnitude, which is what moves it from a truism to a structural vulnerability.

The same gap, seen from the funders' side, is a cross-subsidy (Faulhaber 1975): the roughly $8.4 million paid in 2023 by a handful of industry funders develops rules consumed free by a multi-trillion-dollar network of holders, exchanges, custodians, and ETF issuers who pay nothing. Cross-subsidy of shared infrastructure is rational and often deliberate, and the funders enter it with open eyes. That does not neutralize the dependency it creates, which materialized in the 2023 sustainability scare when the funding base briefly looked like it might contract. The industry funders profiled under Funding the Hand That Merges are the payers. The fragility here is resting a network that size on a set that small.

**The coalition is a minimum-winning set.** Core's position rests on that small funder coalition of roughly four to seven organizations plus node-operator adoption, and coalition theory (Riker 1962) names the exposure: a minimum-winning set has no redundancy, so a single major defection meaningfully degrades developer capacity rather than being absorbed. Both legs show strain - the 2023 scare demonstrated funder-pullback risk, and node operators are already fragmenting toward the Knots implementation. The benign reading has real weight: the funder base has been diversifying with new institutional donors, and node dominance remains above roughly three-quarters, so the coalition is strained rather than near collapse. On balance the fragility is genuine but not acute, and that diversification is what downgraded it - the coalition is exposed to defection rather than visibly failing (medium).

A weaker, related exposure is reputational contagion (Jonsson, Greve and Fujiwara-Greve 2009): reputation-sensitive partners, including funders and ETF issuers whose brands are tied to Core's stewardship, could in principle distance themselves if the schism or the project's documented harassment and doxxing incidents escalated. The same harassment that Evaporative Cooling treats as a drain on capacity is here a latent contagion trigger. The evidence for the channel running live is thin: no funder withdrawal has been observed, and funding is in fact rising, which suggests partners currently read reputational alignment rather than risk. The finding survives only because its trigger genuinely persists - the harassment is documented, so the channel exists even though nothing has yet traveled down it (low).

Who benefits and who pays are almost disjoint sets. The payers are the four-to-seven funders. The beneficiaries are everyone else with a position on the network, and the funding tie running from those latent beneficiaries back to Core is weak to absent: they take the good and return nothing. The clearest cases are the largest holders and issuers, profiled below, whose balance sheets depend on Core continuing to function and whose contribution to it has, until recently, been zero.

The internal power structure is a one-way dependency. Core depends on the funders. The funders, holding an asymmetric option the network's captive users lack, can withdraw or redirect support at will, while users cannot exit cheaply - the asymmetry that Fork or Nothing traces to the absence of any binding arena for contested policy, where disputes resolve by exit rather than voice. The pivotal actor in the current configuration is the broker assembling the largest beneficiaries into a funding bloc, discussed next (medium-high).

The Bitcoin Security Consortium is the pivotal development here and the first real counter-evidence. Announced in July 2026 as a $15 million, three-year commitment from nine of the largest holders, issuers, and custodians - Anchorage, ARK, BlackRock, Block, Blockstream, Coinbase, Fidelity, Galaxy, and Strategy - and coordinated by Brink's Mike Schmidt, it is the clearest instance yet of the largest beneficiaries organizing to fund the good they free-ride on. Its salience is dominant-leaning but self-constrained by design: it disburses independently and disclaims any intent to influence protocol decisions. Those elaborate non-influence disclaimers are the tell - a funding bloc of that concentration does not need formal authority to shape priorities, and the disclaimers signal awareness that it could. It carries an emergent-capture risk because it is structured to look like it carries none (medium).

BlackRock, through its iShares IBIT fund holding roughly 737,413 coins, is a dominant stakeholder shading toward definitive in the consortium and quantum-disclosure window. Its fee stream exists only while Core keeps functioning, and as a consortium co-leader and the issuer whose disclosure choices - repeatedly expanded quantum-risk language - set how the institutional market reads Bitcoin's risk, its leverage is over capital and perception rather than the codebase. It has no authority over what gets merged and does not seek any (medium-high).

Fidelity, through Fidelity Digital Assets and its FBTC fund holding roughly 171,082 coins, is the second-largest spot issuer and the structural exception that self-custodies its own product rather than routing to Coinbase, which makes it the credibility-carrying counter-example to custody concentration. Its incentive is the other issuers' - a free-rider turned funder as a consortium founder - with an added research-as-influence channel. Its salience is dominant.

Strategy, the rebranded MicroStrategy led by Michael Saylor, is the network's largest corporate holder at 843,775 coins, close to four percent of the eventual 21-million supply, and the one actor here whose salience is dangerous: it pairs power and urgency with contested legitimacy. It co-funds through the consortium while running a loud ossification narrative, making it at once a whale free-rider and the ideological leader of the freeze-the-protocol camp, exerting sustained governance pressure with no formal or expert authority over the code - a governance intervention by a non-developer. Its credibility is dented by a 2000 SEC accounting-fraud settlement and a 2024 $40 million District of Columbia tax-fraud settlement, which is why the legitimacy of its pressure is contested rather than accepted (low-medium).

Four further beneficiaries are named but low in salience, each an instance of the same under-provision rather than a distinct dynamic. Binance, the largest exchange holder at roughly 670,000 coins, appears in no funder enumeration - the archetypal free-rider, flagged only for its scale, since free-riding on permissively licensed software is otherwise unremarkable. Tether, whose profit base rests on Bitcoin rails, has made only a token $250,000 OpenSats donation and holds weak Core-specific stake. Galaxy Digital is a consortium founding member whose distinctive trait beyond that membership is thin. The remaining spot-ETF issuers - Grayscale, Bitwise, VanEck, 21Shares, Franklin Templeton, Invesco Galaxy, WisdomTree, Hashdex, Valkyrie, and Morgan Stanley - monetize Core-dependent assets, mostly custody at Coinbase and so reinforce that choke point, and form a reputation-sensitive complementor tail largely redundant with the named leaders (low).

**The trajectory is mixed and, unusually, improving on the axis measured here.** Funding is broadening at the top: the consortium and BlackRock are materializing new provision where there was free-riding, and the diversifying donor base cuts against the fragility finding. The counter-current is that capacity is being consumed as fast as money arrives, people leaving as funding grows, but that drawdown is the subject of Evaporative Cooling and is glossed here only as the offsetting force. On that axis, the provision gap is being addressed for the first time, from the demand side, by the beneficiaries themselves.

Prediction: on a two-to-three-year horizon, a gradual withdrawal by the current funder set is very likely to be met by the largest beneficiaries organizing further provision, the consortium being the first proof that they will (medium). But a sudden withdrawal is only a roughly even chance of being backfilled before developer capacity degrades, because assembling new funding is slower than losing existing funding (low-medium).

Remediation is structurally constrained. Core has no mechanism of its own to convert beneficiaries into funders: it is a public good by construction, with no way to meter or exclude the latent group and no legal entity through which to levy it. The only mechanism now operating on the gap is external and voluntary - the consortium's pooled commitment - which broadens the base but binds no one and disclaims any governance claim, so it mitigates the shortfall without closing it. Within Core's current form, a collective of roughly six key-holders with no treasury of its own, no internal remedy is available. Closing the provision gap depends entirely on beneficiaries continuing to choose to pay (medium).

## 7. Evaporative Cooling: a hostile environment drives out the most capable and normalizes the loss as the cost of the job

Bitcoin Core is losing its most capable maintainers to harassment and burnout faster than it is replacing them, and the surviving cohort has begun to treat those departures as the ordinary cost of the work rather than a warning (high). The pattern is evaporative cooling, and it is the load-bearing dynamic here: it drives the drawdown of the project's accumulated human and reputational capital and deepens a succession void the project never structured itself to close, and it holds whether or not the more contested claims about the surrounding culture are granted.

The mechanism is adverse selection (Akerlof 1970): a hostile environment is a tax on participation that falls hardest on those with the most outside options. The most capable maintainers can leave, and they do. The more captive stay. Each capable exit both lowers the average and hardens the remaining norms, so that survivors read harassment less as a deviation than as the cost of competence. The result is not a one-time loss but a gradient that keeps selecting in the same direction.

The exits are visible and concentrated among high-value contributors. Wladimir van der Laan (laanwj), the former lead maintainer, cited burnout, health, and "constant, bizarre spats on social media" when he stepped back. Gloria Zhao (glozow) resigned and revoked her PGP key in February 2026 after sustained personal attacks, and supporters attribute her departure directly to bullying. Earlier losses fit the same shape - Maxwell in 2015, and others including Falke and Dobson - capable people leaving under pressure while more captive participants remain. A benign reading treats part of this as normal turnover, but harassment- and bullying-driven exits of the most capable are not life-stage attrition.

Behind a stable release surface, the project is consuming its accumulated human and reputational capital rather than compounding it (Mises 1949). Senior maintainers have burned out or been driven off, all-time contributor churn runs at 87.7 percent, the number of active mergers has fallen from eight to five, and no documented succession criteria or process exists. The benign reading holds that steady releases and a deep contributor history signal genuine resilience and ordinary turnover. That reading cannot account for the harassment- and burnout-driven exits or the contraction in merge capacity. (medium-high)

The environment that produces these exits has itself been normalized. Van der Laan has described Bitcoin infrastructure development as "one of the most hostile and crazy working environments in existence," and harassment-driven departures are increasingly treated as the cost of the job rather than a deviation - the drift shifting-baseline syndrome describes (Pauly 1995). This reading is contested and weaker than the exits themselves. An adversarial environment is partly inherent to guarding a trillion-dollar protocol, so some tolerance for conflict is realistic adaptation rather than a lost memory of a healthier norm, and the syndrome's core claim - that the reference point has been forgotten - is partly contradicted because van der Laan can still name the hostility as abnormal. (low-medium)

The culture within which hostility is normalized is insulated. The governing group has been described as "a small group of insiders who were provably hostile to outsiders". A governance study measured 100 percent voting-bloc cohesion between Michael Ford (fanquake) and van der Laan. Dissent during the OP_RETURN dispute was suppressed through deleted or hidden comments and bans - the profile of groupthink (Janis 1972). The benign reading is real: shared technical information genuinely produces convergent conclusions, moderating spam or brigaded pull requests is legitimate rather than censorship, and total cohesion between two long-collaborating leads is consistent with ordinary agreement. What the benign reading cannot settle is whether silent dissenters exist who have learned not to speak. (medium)

The exits land on a structure that was never built to transfer authority (Weber 1978). The lead-maintainer seat has been deliberately vacant since 2023, no documented succession criteria or process exists, and a single maintainer now accounts for 56 to 65 percent of merges - a concentration the project's distributed self-description does not match, and one that a comparable mature project addressed when the Linux kernel adopted a formal written continuity plan in 2026. That concentration is the seam where Evaporative Cooling hands off to One Person Away From a Crisis, which develops the single-merger dependency and the undocumented tacit knowledge no successor has been named to inherit.

The pipeline that might refill the pool is thin (Lave and Wenger 1991). Only one maintainer was added between 2023 and 2026, tenures are long, and the sixteen-year churn record includes 42.5 percent of contributors who appear once and leave. The benign trajectory is visible too: 2025 brought a contributor uptick and a fresh maintainer addition, and long tenure is expected in thankless, specialized volunteer roles. The open question is whether the scarcity of newcomers is hardening the remaining group into orthodoxy. (medium)

The dynamic has no constructive beneficiary inside the project. By the logic of adverse selection, the participants who gain relative standing are the more captive and less capable who remain as abler peers depart. Outside the project, harassment campaigners gain to the extent they succeed in removing their targets, and any party that would prefer a weaker Core benefits from the erosion. What is consumed is paid by the departed maintainers, by the shrinking cohort that absorbs the additional load, and ultimately by the independent node operators and users who depend on the quality of the code these people used to write. (medium)

Internally, the dynamic follows a single fault line between a cohesive inner group and the outsiders it distrusts. The measured cohesion between the two long-tenured leads describes a coalition tight enough that dissent had nowhere neutral to go, and no broker, ombudsman, or moderation function sits between the group and the people it excludes. As capable members exit, the coalition does not broaden but concentrates, which is why hostility, senior attrition, capital consumption, and the succession void deepen together as a self-reinforcing spiral rather than as separate problems. (medium)

**Wladimir van der Laan (laanwj)** was the lead maintainer and is now an honorary referent rather than an active power. His 2023 exit is the cleanest case in the record of stated aim matching behavior: he removed his own merge privileges and named no successor, a genuinely decentralizing act consistent with his stated goal of dispersing governance, driven by burnout, health, and the social-media hostility he named openly. His power base is residual - the reputation and deference his tenure still commands - not control. The one qualification is that the governance study placed his tenure-era influence inside a small, fully cohesive bloc with fanquake, so his decentralizing exit removed a figure who had himself governed from within a narrow coalition.

**Gloria Zhao (glozow)** was the mempool and policy maintainer. She resigned and revoked her PGP key in February 2026. Her exit is the sharpest recent signal of two things at once: the fragility of a single-maintainer-per-domain structure, and harassment operating as a de facto governance weapon. She framed the departure as a "natural stopping point," while supporters framed it as being bullied out, and her 2025 deletion of her X account amid personal attacks corroborates the pressure. The conflict between her and the harassment campaigners who targeted her is the strongest adversarial relationship in this dynamic. Her diagnostic value as an exit-and-succession signal is high and her hidden-power risk is low. The mempool-policy vacuum her departure opened is developed by One Person Away From a Crisis. (medium)

**Peter Todd** is a foundational contributor - author of BIP 65 (CLTV) and co-author of BIP 125 (RBF) - whose agenda-setting reach exceeds his non-maintainer standing: he seeded the OP_RETURN relaxation years before it shipped, a lineage The v30 Concession develops. He appears here for a different reason. Named in an October 2024 HBO documentary as the alleged inventor of Bitcoin, a claim he calls "ludicrous," he became an involuntary exemplar of the community-health and harassment cost examined here - a reputational lightning rod whose safety exposure was imposed on him rather than sought. On the technical axis he is dominant. On the safety axis he is dependent, carrying a harm he did not choose. (medium)

**Cullen Hoback**, director of the October 2024 HBO documentary "Money Electric," is the proximate source of the naming that drove the harassment and safety exposure examined here. The amplification reading is contested: the film is a one-time press event, and treating journalism as an ongoing stakeholder in the project's health overstates a role it does not durably hold. (low)

**Samson Mow (JAN3)** appears as a prominent figure cited amplifying Core-versus-Knots hostility and featured in the doxxing narrative. The classification is genuinely ambiguous, because the line between a vocal partisan and a harassment amplifier is not one the available evidence draws cleanly. (low)

The **social-media hostility vectors** around the OP_RETURN dispute are a positional actor rather than a named one: sustained anonymous attacks - the "Knotzis" and "Toddler" epithets, deleted comments - that drove Zhao off X and are credited with contributing to her exit. Their identity is positional because it is the function, not any single account, that matters. The benign caveat is that some of this is ambient hostility inherent to high-stakes public debate, and the specific exit attribution rests on a single perspective. (low-medium)

The trajectory across these findings is downward and self-reinforcing: the culture is stable, but hostility, senior exits, capital consumption, and the succession void are all worsening together, with only a single 2025 maintainer addition keeping the pipeline nominally flat. The forecast turns on whether the surviving cohort still holds a reference point for a healthy norm. Over the next one to two years, and absent any neutral mechanism to absorb harassment, the remaining maintainers likely recalibrate their expectations downward and come to read continued degradation as normal - the point at which lost talent stops registering as loss. Confidence is medium: the exits themselves are not in doubt, but the internal recalibration is inferred from a stable-culture signal set against contested normalization findings. The competing outcome, in which a retained memory of a healthier baseline lets the cohort resist the drift, remains live, since van der Laan still describes that hostility as abnormal, but it is weakening (low-medium).

No neutral community-health or moderation function exists to absorb any of this. Bitcoin Core has no HR, no ombudsman, and no neutral moderation body, and the persistent absence is what lets harassment convert directly into burnout and exit. The remedy is scoped to a small volunteer project's means - not a staffed HR department, but the kind of lightweight, delegated function comparable mature projects adopted: the Linux Foundation's code of conduct and the Rust project's moderation team both created a place for this class of harm to go. Standing up an equivalent is within Core's governance form and budget. The binding constraint is will rather than resources. (medium-high)

## 8. One Person Away From a Crisis: continuity rests on a single 56-65% merger and undocumented tacit knowledge with no named successor

Bitcoin Core's operational continuity rests on a single dominant merger who handles 56-65% of all merges and on undocumented tacit knowledge concentrated in a handful of contributors, with no named successor and no mechanism to appoint one (high). That dependency is a continuity exposure, not merely a skill shortage: the concentration produces fast, high-quality output today, but the project holds no plan to transfer either the merge role or the embodied knowledge if the people carrying them leave.

**The dynamic** is a single bus factor wearing three faces. Its human-capital face is undocumented tacit knowledge held by a few. Its operational face is the one dominant merger at 56-65% with no succession. It surfaces at specific links where provision is thin, most concretely mempool policy. These are not three problems but one dependency seen from three sides - the knowledge concentration and the merge concentration are the same bus factor's human and operational halves, and the weakest-link exposure is that bus factor materializing at a named link. The trajectory across all three is deepening, and none of the three is internally contested.

The single-stakeholder dependency (Chopra and Sodhi 2004) is the dominant thread: the project runs on multiple single points of dependence - GitHub as sole code-hosting platform (project-flagged), the external Guix build farm, and above all the single dominant merger, Michael Ford (fanquake), whose grip on the merge key is anatomized in the Janitor's Grip, at 56-65% of merges. The benign reading is that consolidating on one high-quality host and one prolific merger is convenient and partly mitigated by mirrors, PGP-signed merges, and forkability. But those mitigations protect code integrity, not continuity of the merge process, and the single-merger bus factor is unmitigated by any succession plan. On continuity, the evidence favors the exposure reading.

Underneath sits tacit-knowledge leverage (Polanyi 1966): function depends heavily on knowledge that is undocumented and embodied. Brink frames the dominant merger as the project's "institutional memory," Pieter Wuille (sipa) holds near-unmatched protocol and cryptography expertise, and the single-person merge bottleneck is the most-cited bus-factor concern in the record. Concentrated tacit expertise can reflect genuine skill scarcity rather than deliberate hoarding, but a 16-year-mature project retaining a single-person bus factor with no documented succession is a deviation the "expected at younger scale" reading cannot cover. Because the knowledge is embodied rather than written, succession is intrinsically hard, and the pool from which a successor would be drawn is itself draining (Evaporative Cooling).

The exposure has already materialized where provision is weakest-link (Hirshleifer 1983): a single unreviewed consensus bug or stale dependency can split the chain, as the 2013 BerkeleyDB/LevelDB split and the near-split from OpenSSL signature parsing both showed, and Brink's own analysis names consensus fragility and supply-chain risk as the motivation for dependency removal. Uneven review distribution is the FOSS baseline, and Core actively hardens links through dependency removal, libsecp256k1, and the 2025 audit. But the 2013 split did materialize, and mempool policy is under-provisioned today, having lost its maintainer (that departure is treated in Evaporative Cooling) (medium-high).

**The near-term beneficiaries** are the ecosystem, which gets high merge throughput and deep expertise at low coordination cost, and the dominant contributors themselves, who accrue leverage - recognized or not - from being effectively irreplaceable. The cost falls on every independent node operator and the wider ecosystem downstream of Core, which carry an unfunded continuity risk: a sudden exit would strand the merge process and its embodied knowledge with no transfer path. The dependency concentrates on two people: the ecosystem leans heavily on Wuille's expertise, and Core leans heavily on the dominant merger's throughput, while Core itself depends moderately on Blockstream's libsecp256k1, a Wuille design. The fault line is the absence of any process that would convert these individual dependencies into transferable institutional capacity. The one visible succession signal is a named candidate positioned at the weakest link, which suggests the binding constraint is at least recognized there.

Wuille is the dominant actor in this dynamic and edges toward definitive on security-critical matters. His power base is expert power that exceeds any formal authority: he surrendered maintainership in July 2022, yet his designs remain load-bearing - libsecp256k1 underlies every signature path - his pre-disclosure CVE access confers early leverage over security-critical fixes, and his research posts, from post-quantum migration to EC-disabling triggers, set direction. His influence is durable because it rests on expertise rather than a revocable role, so he stands to lose little and the project stands to lose much if he steps back. The flag to carry forward is that Blockstream co-ownership and Chaincode employment place commercial interests behind a nominally neutral expert, though observed behavior is neutral (high on influence; medium on the commercial-conflict caveat).

Antoine Poinsot (Chaincode Labs) is a dependent stakeholder trending dominant. A Chaincode-funded, non-maintainer security and policy contributor - author of BIP 54 Consensus Cleanup, reporter of CVE-2024-38365, and a first-audit collaborator - he is positioned to backfill the mempool and policy-validation agenda-setting role that Gloria Zhao (glozow) vacated in early 2026. Proposal authorship and audit access already give him roadmap influence exceeding his formal, no-commit-bit status, the signature of a dependent actor acquiring dominant traits. Two latent vectors bear watching: his Chaincode employment, shared with Wuille, concentrates policy expertise in a single funder, and a Wizardsardine shareholding is a commercial interest one step removed from his protocol positions (medium).

The third actor is not a person but an absence: no documented succession or continuity mechanism exists around the 56-65% bus factor, and the lead-maintainer role has been deliberately vacant since 2023. Whether this absence is pathology or lean governance is genuinely contested, and the benign reading has weight - minimalism can be appropriate for a volunteer project, which softens "void as pathology" toward "lean governance." On the narrow question of continuity, though, the comparison cuts the other way: Linux formalized written continuity in 2026 and Core did not, which makes the absence a real gap rather than merely a distributed-model choice (medium).

**Trajectory is deepening** as merge concentration has risen and the mempool link opened on a maintainer's exit, with no offsetting succession mechanism. Over the next 24 months, conditional on the sudden departure of the dominant merger or a key knowledge-holder, an orderly handoff of the merge role and its embodied knowledge is unlikely, since no mechanism exists to execute one. Confidence in that judgment is medium-high. The standalone probability of such a departure inside the window is itself low, but the concentration means the cost lands undiffused if it comes. Whether the dominant stakeholder recognizes the leverage the position confers is unmeasured, and that unknown decides whether this bus factor is an accident awaiting a trigger or an asset quietly held. Confidence on the leverage question is low.

The remediation is a mechanism Core lacks rather than an inadequate existing one: a documented succession-and-continuity plan that names and trains a standby merger and transfers embodied merge-and-review knowledge before an exit rather than after it. Such a mechanism is well within reach of a volunteer project on Core's budget and governance form, with Linux's 2026 written-continuity formalization as a proximate model. The contested downgrade tempers the prescription - if the minimalism is deliberate, the adequate step may be modest, documenting the tacit knowledge and naming a standby rather than importing heavier governance - but no such mechanism exists today (medium).

## 9. The v30 Concession: whoever held the merge settled the OP_RETURN fight and called it consensus, stranding a quarter of nodes onto Knots

The v30 relaxation of Bitcoin Core's OP_RETURN relay limit was settled over sustained objection by the holders of the merge keys, ratified as consensus, and in the process stranded roughly a quarter of nodes onto Bitcoin Knots. (medium) The episode is the clearest single instance here of the pattern documented in the Janitor's Grip - merge-key holders resolving contested consensus - operating on a live decision rather than in the abstract: control of the merge, uncontested as a fact, converted a disputed relay-policy change into a shipped default. What makes it diagnostic is not the technical merits of the change but the sequence by which a disputed change became a settled one and the exit that sequence produced.

The mechanism runs in three linked moves. A relay-policy default that concentrates value on a narrow class of users was adopted despite objection, the relaxation itself originating upstream of the merge with a contributor treated under Evaporative Cooling. The maintainer holding merge authority then merged a concession pull request (PR #33453) in the hours before the v30 release window, an action that partially defused the dispute while leaving the relaxation in place. Finally, the outcome was characterized as consensus, after which the operators who lost the argument migrated rather than continued to contest it. Each move depends on the one before it, and the concentration of merge authority that makes the first possible is the same concentration profiled in the Janitor's Grip. (medium)

The dominant reading of the change is a concentrated-benefits, diffuse-costs transfer (Wilson 1980): the relaxation concentrates benefit on data-embedding and inscription users while diffusing storage and relay burden, and a contested loss of monetary purity, across the whole population of node operators, who were unorganized until Knots gave them a vehicle. The commercial funders of that demand take a concentrated complementary benefit while the centralization risk stays diffuse. The benign reading is that this benefit-to-cost shape describes most institutional defaults and here reflects an ordinary relay-policy choice rather than a targeted transfer. What tilts the weight toward the first reading is the scale of the response: a routine default does not usually move a quarter of nodes. (low-medium)

The same change read ideologically rather than materially is an instance of mission drift (Grimes et al. 2019): critics frame the relaxation as a symbolic abandonment of Bitcoin's monetary-purity founding idea even as the founding language is retained. The competing reading is that a relay change with a defensible technical rationale is ordinary adaptation contested on ideological grounds. On this record the drift reading is the weakest of the four and is better understood as the ideological face of the same transfer than as an independent dynamic. (low)

The procedural cover for the outcome is rough-consensus laundering (Russell 2006) - the consensus label used to ratify a maintainer-preferred contested result. The supporting evidence is specific: inconsistent application of the project's own no-contentious-changes standard, comments deleted or hidden, and objections bypassed rather than answered, followed by the mass exit to Knots. The benign reading carries real force - judged rather than counted consensus is the legitimate norm for this class of project, and the maintainers answered dissent with a concession rather than ignoring it. The open question is whether overruled dissenters retain any recourse short of forking. The migration to Knots suggests they concluded they did not. (medium)

The fallout is a network-effects coordination failure in its excess-momentum form (Farrell and Saloner 1985). The framework's better-known failure mode is excess inertia, beneficial upgrades stalling on coordination cost, as when only about 55 percent of nodes enforced Taproot months after activation. Its mirror, excess momentum, is what materialized here, as labeling the contested merge consensus drove roughly a quarter of nodes - about 22 to 24 percent - onto Knots instead of resolving the dispute. The benign reading is that deep conservatism is the right baseline for consensus-critical software, so most apparent inertia is safety. That reading explains the inertia half but not the stranding. (low-medium)

**Benefit and cost** divide along a clean line: the concentrated beneficiaries are data-embedding and inscription users and the firms that monetize their demand, while the diffuse cost-bearers are independent node operators (collective), who absorb storage and relay burden and the contested purity loss without ever having organized around the decision. The party that captured the benefit never formally participated in the governance that delivered it, and the party that bore the cost could register itself only by leaving. (low-medium)

Two named relationships define the internal power map, with a third forming outside it. The sharpest is a strong, standing conflict between Bitcoin Knots / Luke Dashjr and the Bitcoin Core maintainers (collective): Knots is at once the destination of the exit and the organized opposition to the relay policy, which makes the migration self-reinforcing rather than a one-time protest. The second is the moderate information flow from Ava Chow (achow101) to the ecosystem, since the actor who took the pivotal merge also shapes how the decision is explained through education and coordination roles reaching well past the wallet code the merge touched. Outside the arena the beneficiaries form a loose coalition - the demand's originator, the dominant marketplace, and the exchanges above it - none holding a governance seat yet all gaining from the outcome. (medium)

Ava Chow (achow101) is the definitive stakeholder of this dynamic, holding power, urgency, and legitimacy in the one action that decided it. She took the single pivotal step, merging the concession pull request in the hours before the release window, that partially defused the schism, and her influence extends beyond Core's wallet code through the 2024 BIP-editor expansion she coordinated, the co-founding of Localhost Research, and an education-and-narrative role that supplies the moderate information flow noted above. Her power base is authority over merges compounded by technical reputation rather than any formal office. Alignment is genuinely contested: she signed the 2025 permissive relay-policy statement, later NACKed a status-quo-restoring pull request (PR #33682), and merged the concession - a sequence detractors read as inconsistent with the no-contentious-changes standard and supporters read as pragmatic de-escalation. Which reading holds turns on which side of the schism the observer occupies. (medium)

Casey Rodarmor is a dangerous stakeholder - high power and urgency, low legitimacy - sitting entirely outside Core's arena. As the creator of Ordinals and Runes he manufactured the data-embedding demand behind the contested relaxation: launching Runes at the April 2024 halving drove OP_RETURN output volume up more than tenfold. The launch was agenda-setting from outside by a single external protocol author with no governance authority, who by self-excluding from Core's process leaves the maintainers able only to react to what he ships. His trajectory is re-energizing rather than fading, which keeps pressure on the same relay margin. (medium)

UniSat (UniPro Technology) is the second dangerous stakeholder: the largest Ordinals and Runes marketplace, at roughly 25 million users and about $1.2 billion in volume, and a principal beneficiary of a Core decision it never formally influenced. Its cap table pushes the benefit upward to major exchanges behind a neutral-infrastructure presentation, and its contributor role on a Core-codebase fork gives that commercial bloc a parallel governance surface should Core's arena stay closed to it. Its position is consolidating as the marketplace tier concentrates. (low-medium)

Two further beneficiaries are real but low in salience. Xverse / OKX Wallet / Oyl are wallets monetizing the same demand, tail beneficiaries of the change, largely redundant with UniSat and diminished after Magic Eden's exit in March 2026. Marathon Digital (MARA) / Slipstream is a public miner whose direct-submission infrastructure for large-data transactions is a distinct data-embedding role predating v30, surviving here only on that narrow distinction. (low)

The event is entrenched: the change shipped and has not been reversed, the concession did not restore the prior default, and the underlying demand keeps intensifying while merge concentration deepens. The unresolved question is the one named by the transfer reading - whether the cost-bearing operators come to understand the relay default as a subsidy they underwrite rather than a neutral technical choice. Over the next 12 to 18 months, continued migration toward Knots is likely rather than a reversal to Core's defaults, conditional on that awareness hardening. If it stalls, the exit plateaus near its current share. The mechanism behind that forecast, operator awareness of the transfer, is also its weakest link. (low-medium)

This assessment identifies no remediation path for the dynamic. The organization has no existing mechanism scoped to route this class of concern - a contested relay-policy default ratified as consensus - and the record names no specific absent mechanism to recommend within Core's current governance form. The exit to Knots is operating as the de facto substitute for a remedy, which is the subject of Fork or Nothing.

## 10. Governing Without a Mandate: maintainers act for a base that never chose them and cannot remove them, on legitimacy borrowed from lineage and a first audit

Bitcoin Core's maintainers exercise governing authority over a base that never selected them and cannot remove them except by forking, and they sustain that authority on a legitimacy renewed by shipped code and Satoshi lineage rather than conferred by any mandate (medium). Contested legitimacy is the hub of this dynamic: the representation gap and the proxy problem erode it, the absence of any accountable board removes the check that would ordinarily constrain it, and a borrowed procedural form hollows it from within, even as functional performance and a first external audit renew it on a separate measure. The result is not an authority that is failing but one whose basis is quietly shifting - from consent toward incumbency - while the structure that would register that shift does not exist.

The mechanism is a closed proxy loop. The parties who bear the consequences of consensus decisions hold no governance seat, so the maintainers are necessarily proxies for a group that neither chose them nor can recall them. With no board or accountable external check, responsibility diffuses into the accountability sink analyzed under the Janitor's Grip, where no individual is answerable. That unaccountable proxy authority is, in turn, why the project's legitimacy is increasingly contested and must lean on incumbency and lineage. Running alongside the loop is a divergence best read as two separate measures. Core's functional and resource inputs - shipped releases, a first audit, broadening funding - are improving, while its procedural inputs are static to eroding, so legitimacy is being renewed and hollowed at once, along different dimensions, with the net direction down (low-medium).

The representation gap is the strongest structural finding here (Young 2000). The parties who bear the consequences of consensus decisions - hundreds of millions of holders and users, and the broad population of node operators - hold no governance seat and participate only through node-voting or exit. The benign reading is real: no open-source project seats every affected party, and node-voting or forking is the intended proxy channel. Two features make the gap unusual rather than ordinary, though. The affected population is a financial stakeholder base in the hundreds of millions rather than a typical user community. Pure holders who run no node have no node-voting channel at all, leaving sale as their only instrument (medium-high).

From that gap follows the proxy problem (Pitkin 1967). The maintainers effectively act for a base that did not appoint them and cannot remove them short of a fork, and at least one analyst observes that they "claim to represent the will of the people in some vague Rousseauian sense" while disclaiming responsibility for outcomes. Appointed technical stewardship is a legitimate governance design, and forkability is a genuine if blunt removal mechanism. The "will of the people" characterization rests on a single analyst's phrasing. The finding survives on the structural fact - representation without selection or recall - rather than on the rhetoric (medium).

Core has no board (Tillotson and Tropman 2025). Its de facto governing body is the self-perpetuating maintainer group, admitted by co-optation and subject to no accountable external check, which is a board-capture analog rather than a literal captured board. The framing has a clear limit, and it should be conceded: no board exists to be captured, self-perpetuating selection is the statutory baseline for many nonprofit forms, and adjacent funders deliberately diffuse control through conventional boards. The concentration the analogy points at is real, but it is captured better by the merge-key concentration analyzed under the Janitor's Grip. Here the finding contributes only the narrower point that the governing body has no external body above it to answer to (low-medium).

On the security side, decision-relevant information is concentrated and has been actively withheld. In July 2024 six contributors admitted the project "has historically done a poor job at publicly disclosing security-critical bugs," called the perception that Core was bug-free "dangerous and... not accurate," and began releasing long-embargoed vulnerabilities, among them CVE-2024-52912, fixed in 2021 and disclosed only in 2024. Consensus-critical knowledge is likewise held by a few, with security researchers routing pre-disclosure vulnerabilities to Pieter Wuille (sipa). Embargoing a vulnerability until fixes propagate is ordinary responsible-disclosure practice. A three-year post-fix embargo, paired with the project's own "dangerous" self-assessment, is not.

The governance side is the same gap seen from oversight (Ashby 1956). Independent scrutiny was minimal until recently: Core's first public third-party security audit occurred only in November 2025, performed by Quarkslab and funded through Brink, and governance research reports a 26.5 percent maintainer self-merge rate, meaning review substantially by the reviewed. The self-correction is genuine and the trajectory is improving, which the finding concedes. But the self-merge rate is a present structural gap, and the reading that external audit was simply unaffordable is strained for a project whose resources are documented under A Trillion-Dollar Good on an $8 Million Subsidy (medium).

These inputs converge on legitimacy, which organizes the dynamic (Suchman 1995). Core's standing rests partly on incumbency and Satoshi lineage and is renewed by shipping - the v31.0 release, the 2025 audit - yet it was, in the project's own recent history, "fiercely criticized and challenged" through the v30 cycle and is labeled "highly centralised... undemocratic... technocratic" in academic work. The benign reading is that institutional standing legitimately outlasts any single performance period and that Core keeps earning it through shipped releases and its first external audit. Both readings survive, which is why the verdict is contested rather than settled. What tips the balance is that split: the functional and resource renewals are real, but they are set against procedural standing that is eroding. The consensus-laundering examined under the v30 Concession depletes that procedural standing, as does the project's borrowed form (DiMaggio and Powell 1983) - Core adopts the IETF "rough consensus" label and an RFC-style proposal process but, critics argue, took the vocabulary without the IETF's procedural safeguards: a neutral chair, chartered working groups, a fixed last-call, an appeals path. The borrowing may be substantive rather than ceremonial if rough consensus genuinely fits a permissionless project better than formal voting, and that reading survives. But on the contested reading the form supplies the appearance of procedural legitimacy without its constraints, and the same borrowed-form problem reappears in the role-conversion argument developed under the Janitor's Grip (low-medium).

The arrangement's beneficiaries are the incumbent maintainers, who retain broad discretion with no body above them to answer to, and - on the functional side - the network itself, which receives conservative, competent stewardship. Those who pay are the unrepresented: the holders and node operators who bear consensus outcomes without a seat, and most acutely the pure holders who run no node and therefore hold neither voice nor vote, only the option to sell. The gap is structural rather than incidental, because there is no defined membership, electorate, or accountable body through which the consequence-bearing base could register a preference, so the cost of exclusion falls on those least able to organize against it (low-medium).

The internal power relations are lopsided along information lines. The flow from security researchers to Pieter Wuille (sipa) is strong and privileged - pre-disclosure vulnerabilities move to a small trusted set - while the flow from the maintainers back to users and the network is only moderate, mediated by release notes and embargo timing. That asymmetry is the lever: whoever holds the concentrated information sets the terms on which the base can react. The one countervailing development is external and recent - an independent oversight niche that sat empty for most of Core's history has now been occupied by auditors and quantitative researchers, introducing a check the structure never generated internally (medium).

Quarkslab is the most salient external actor in this dynamic: it holds both legitimacy and real power but presses no urgent claim, which places it in the dominant tier. It is a security-research firm, and its formal role here is narrow - it performed Core's first-ever public third-party security audit in 2025, coordinated by OSTIF and funded through Brink, filling the oversight niche that had been empty. Its power base is not positional but epistemic, resting on expert and informational power, because by certifying Core as mature and conservatively managed it shapes the security narrative that funders, press, and regulators rely on. What it wants is reputational: audits are its product and its credibility is the asset at stake. Its exposure to capture is mild - it is paid through the Brink and OSTIF network and was acquired by Airbus in 2026 - but it carries the lowest hidden-influence risk of the external actors here, and its trajectory is that of a niche now occupied and, in principle, repeatable.

The quantitative researcher behind the maintainer-capture dataset - publishing pseudonymously as bitcoin-governance-research and through BCAP - is lower in formal standing but disproportionately influential, a dependent stakeholder trending toward discretionary. Its power base is purely informational: it produced the governance metrics on which the maintainer-capture narrative rests, including the maintainer self-merge rate cited above, and those figures propagate largely unchallenged because no competing dataset exists. Its influence therefore depends on remaining the sole source and would fade the moment a rival dataset appeared. Two flags qualify the reliance: the researcher operates a competing node implementation, The Bitcoin Commons, whose interest is not disclosed in the framing of the metrics, and single-source concentration means the capture narrative rests on one unaudited hand. The trajectory is uncertain by construction, because the actor is pseudonymous and its incentives cannot be fully read.

OSTIF, the Open Source Technology Improvement Fund, is the constructive occupant of the same oversight niche, and its salience is low-to-medium and contested. Its role was coordination: it organized Core's first public third-party audit, working on Brink's behalf but as a distinct organization, and its engagement to date is essentially singular. It wants under-audited open-source infrastructure reviewed. Its gain or loss is reputational and modest, and its trajectory is flat unless the audit relationship recurs.

The academic governance critics - among them De Filippi and scholars publishing through Oxford, the Stanford Journal of Blockchain Law and Policy, and Walch - supply the scholarship behind the "undemocratic" and "technocratic" verdicts that feed the contested-legitimacy finding. Their salience is low-to-medium and contested, and their role is partly positional: they are external observers whose influence is the epistemic weight of their characterizations, grounded in the findings' own citations rather than in any lever over the project. They want accurate characterization and, in some cases, reform. They stand to gain or lose only standing, and their pressure is stable rather than rising.

BitMEX Research is a longstanding independent analyst of Bitcoin governance, cited here as external research and distinct from BitMEX's historical role in funding development. It is not part of the formal register. Its salience is low-to-medium and contested, its power base informational, and its trajectory stable - a persistent outside voice rather than a decisive one.

Two stakeholders in this dynamic are defined by position rather than identity. The unorganized retail holders and the passive base that now holds Bitcoin through exchange-traded products are hundreds of millions of parties who bear consensus consequences with no governance seat. The pure holders among them, who run no node, have literally no channel, and the advocates who might speak for them disclaim doing so, so the representation gap persists here as an absence rather than a grievance, its unusual feature being scale. Beneath them is a deeper void: there is no defined membership, electorate, or accountable body at all. That absence is what makes both a structureless governing elite and the accountability sink analyzed under the Janitor's Grip possible, because nothing exists that could be captured or formally held to account (low-medium).

The trajectory is a structure holding still while its legitimacy erodes. The proxy arrangement, the representation gap, and the absent board are all stable. What moves is legitimacy, and it moves down, only partly offset by the functional and resource renewals counted on the other side. The governing question for the horizon is what holds stakeholders to Core as active consent thins, and the most likely answer is not renewed deference but its substitutes: switching costs, network dependency, and the sheer difficulty of coordinating an exit. Absent an external shock - a contested consensus change large enough to make forking worth its cost - stakeholder adherence over the next two to three years will very likely rest on inertia and dependency rather than on a renewed mandate, with the maintainers retaining authority because leaving is expensive rather than because the base re-legitimates them (low-medium). Whether the accountability-defeating void is engineered or emergent is the slower question. The evidence favors emergent - a structure inherited from Satoshi's departure and the project's volunteer origins rather than deliberately built to defeat oversight - though it is now load-bearing and unlikely to be filled voluntarily by those it protects (low).

No remediation mechanism currently exists for this dynamic, and the assessment routes no specific concern to it. The gap the findings identify is the absence of any accountable channel - no board above the maintainers, no defined electorate, no binding arena in which the consequence-bearing base could register a preference short of forking. What Core could adopt within its permissionless form, its volunteer-and-grant governance, and its lack of any defined membership is correspondingly narrow: not a board or an election, which the form cannot support, but lighter accountability instruments that fit it - a recurring external-audit cadence rather than a single engagement, a published successor and merge-key custody plan, and a standing independent review function that converts one-time oversight into a repeatable check. None of these closes the mandate gap. They only make the exercise of unmandated authority more legible. The binding channel that would actually close it - exit to a maturing alternative - is developed under Fork or Nothing (low-medium).

## 11. Fork or Nothing: with voice suppressed and no binding arena, dissent can only exit, and the alternatives are finally maturing.

At Bitcoin Core, serious consensus disputes have no binding channel of resolution: voice is thin, no low-cost arena can settle a substantive disagreement, and dissent can therefore be registered only by exit or fork, a lever now sharpened by maturing independent alternatives (medium). The condition is not captive silence but its structural inverse, a permissionless system that privileges exit over voice by design, in which whoever holds the status quo prevails by default while objectors are left to leave. What has changed is the credibility of leaving: the exit options are now real enough to invert the relationship they were built to escape.

The dynamic proceeds in three linked movements (Hirschman 1970). Voice fails first: during the OP_RETURN dispute, the episode anatomized in the v30 Concession, objections were overridden, comments deleted, dissenters banned, and the change merged. Denied an effective say, dissatisfied operators took the second movement, exit, migrating to Bitcoin Knots. Because forkability is the designed safety valve, that exit is never blocked. The third movement is a structural absence - there is no binding low-cost arena in which the dispute could have been decided on any terms other than who controlled the merge - so exit and fork are not a breakdown of the process but the process's only terminal step. (medium)

The evidence for thin voice is the migration itself. Following the OP_RETURN dispute, roughly 22 to 24 percent of reachable nodes moved to Knots, a quarter of the network exiting rather than accepting the merged outcome. The reading is contested and the contest matters. On the malign account, the migration is a last-resort defection forced by a process that left no room for objection. On the benign account, open-source forkability is the intended voice-via-exit mechanism, and a live, well-populated alternative is the system working as designed rather than trapping anyone. The evidence does not settle which frame is correct, because both predict the same observable - a large, orderly exit - and permissionless systems can be argued to privilege exit over voice by construction. What the migration does establish, on either reading, is that voice was insufficient to hold the dissatisfied inside the existing implementation. (medium)

That exit is not equally available to everyone (Fisher and Ury 1981). The alternatives are sharply asymmetric. Node operators held a ready substitute in Knots and used it, which gave them real leverage. Developers hold leverage through the existence of multiple funders. End users and holders, by contrast, are largely captive, because there is no near substitute for Bitcoin-plus-Core that a holder can switch to without leaving the asset class, so their apparent loyalty is not a revealed preference. The benign reading is that captivity of end users is inherent to any dominant standard and does not imply the standard-setter exploits them, and that the record is largely descriptive, showing no evidence captive users are actively taken for granted. The asymmetry is the leverage story here: the constituencies that can leave shape outcomes, and the constituency that cannot is shaped by them. (low-medium)

The absence that forces exit is the missing arena (Ostrom 1990). Low-cost venues exist for routine disputes - the mailing list, IRC, CoreDev, and Delving Bitcoin - and they resolve most everyday questions. But serious substantive conflicts have repeatedly terminated only in exit or fork: Bitcoin Cash in 2017 and the Knots migration in 2025. The pattern indicates that no binding low-cost arena exists for contested policy. When a dispute is fundamental, there is no forum whose decision the losing side is obligated to accept. The benign reading holds that forkability is the designed dispute-resolution mechanism of a permissionless system, and that the everyday venues carry the ordinary load, so whether the fork-only terminal path is a defect or the intended design is the live contest rather than a settled finding. Either way the direction is the same: absent a binding arena, whoever controls the status quo benefits, because inertia decides what a forum otherwise would. (medium)

Power in this system tracks the dependent party's lack of alternatives (Emerson 1962). Users depend on Core for the de facto specification, developers depend on a small set of roughly four to seven funders for salary, and Core itself depends on GitHub for hosting, a dependency the project has flagged as a single point of failure with no ready substitute. Where a dependent builds an alternative, the relation inverts: node operators reduced their dependence and inverted the power balance through the same migration to Knots. Where none exists, the dependence persists unaltered - the hosting dependency has not moved, a fragility whose fuller treatment belongs to Borrowed Ground. Both readings agree on these facts and differ only on framing: the malign frame reads engineered leverage, the benign frame reads the ordinary, un-engineered dependence of specialized software that self-corrects once a dependent invests in an alternative, and no evidence of deliberately engineered leverage is shown. (medium)

Core's structural position is that of the dominant net provider of the consensus reference on which the entire ecosystem depends (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978), so a failure at Core would cascade network-wide. The finding is moderated by its own remedy: dependents are actively building the alternatives - Knots, btcd, libbitcoinkernel, and Floresta - that partly route around the position and reduce the cascade risk. The benign reading is that being the load-bearing reference is the inherent and legitimate function of a reference implementation, and that the alternatives-building materially lowers the concentration it describes. The bottom line, then, is that Core is dominant while the exits mature: the position and the routes around it are advancing at the same time. (low-medium)

The beneficiaries of this configuration are the constituencies with a credible exit and the holder of the status quo. Node operators and Bitcoin Knots converted a ready alternative into leverage and forced a concession from the maintainer collective. The maintainers, in turn, benefit from the absence of a binding arena, since without one the merged status quo stands unless a costly fork dislodges it. Those who pay are the captive constituencies, end users and holders, who have neither an effective voice nor an exit that does not mean leaving the asset, and who therefore absorb whatever the contest between the exit-capable parties produces. The fork-or-nothing structure also imposes a diffuse cost on the whole system, because it is the same defection mechanism that leaves the funding coalition one departure from a scare, a fragility that A Trillion-Dollar Good on an $8 Million Subsidy treats in full. (medium)

The internal power map has a clear broker and clear fault lines. Bitcoin Knots and Luke Dashjr sit at the center as the broker of conservative opposition: node operators cooperate with Knots strongly, and Knots exerts strong political pressure on the maintainers, while node operators themselves stand in strong conflict with the maintainers over contested changes. A second axis runs through the mining layer, where the pools apply strong political pressure on protocol activation and stand in moderate conflict with node operators, and where Ocean, tied to the Knots camp, is in moderate conflict with the rest of the pools, so the mining bloc is not unified. Beneath all of it is the ecosystem's strong dependency on Core, the resource-dependence relation that gives the position its weight and gives the maintainers, through the merge seat treated in The Janitor's Grip, the ability to settle a contested question without a mandate. (medium)

**Bitcoin Knots and Luke Dashjr** are the definitive stakeholder of this dynamic, sitting at the boundary between definitive and dangerous because legitimacy is the softest of their attributes. Knots is the live exit option, holding roughly 22 to 24 percent of reachable nodes, and its influence exceeds even that share: Dashjr sets the terms of the conservative opposition, historically monopolized BIP-number assignment, and through the Ocean mining pool can enforce filtered block templates at the mining layer. Ocean itself was seed-funded at roughly 6.2 million dollars by Jack Dorsey's Block with Tether-linked hashrate, a proxy linkage back to Spiral, Block, and Dorsey. Dashjr is the primary counter-power to the maintainer collective and the party that forced Core's v30 concession. The power is real and demonstrated, and its trajectory is a plateau rather than a further rise. (medium)

**Independent node operators** are the enforcement layer and the ultimate adoption-and-exit check, the constituency whose refusal defeated SegWit2x. Their power is definitive in aggregate but latent, diffuse, and leaderless, and it is harder to measure than it looks: the node-vote metric is manipulable and Sybil-susceptible, the trackers became contested after Bitnodes went dark, and Dashjr operates one of the surviving crawlers. Real sovereign-economic weight therefore sits not with raw node counts but with the subset of nodes tied to economic activity - exchanges, custodians, and merchants - whose exit would actually move the network. (low-medium)

**Mining pools** are a dangerous stakeholder, high in power and urgency but contested in legitimacy. They hold de facto control over consensus activation through signaling, with the top three pools exceeding roughly 55 to 63 percent of blocks, and that control is obscured by hidden concentration behind pool brands: AntPool is Bitmain, Foundry is DCG, and Ocean is tied to Dashjr, Block, and Tether. Displayed pool share further masks the operator discretion exercised over aggregated hashrate. The pools are active in the live BIP-110 fight, whose window falls around August 2026, where a single Foundry internal vote could swing the tally - the near-term test of whether the mining layer resolves a contested question by voice or by the exit-and-fork path this dynamic describes. (medium)

Three independent node implementations make the collective exit credible and carry contested-low salience individually. **btcd and btcsuite** is an independent Go full node at roughly 0.05 percent of reachable share that underpins the Lightning implementation LND. Its tiny share limits its leverage, and it reads as a constructive diversity actor rather than a threat. **Floresta and Davidson Souza** is a Rust, Utreexo-based compact-state node that lowers the cost of running a validating node and diversifies implementation risk. It is OpenSats-funded but organizationally distinct, and because it wraps libbitcoinkernel it remains partly dependent on Core. **libbitcoin and Eric Voskuil** is a long-running independent C++ node and library stack that contributes to client diversity, though its low adoption similarly limits its leverage. None individually constrains Core. Together they are the maturing ecosystem that turns the collective exit into a real option rather than a threat. (low)

The **absent binding conflict-resolution arena** is a positional stakeholder, an actor defined by a structural gap rather than a named identity. Because no low-cost binding arena exists for contested policy, serious disputes resolve only by exit or fork, as Bitcoin Cash in 2017 and the Knots migration in 2025 both show, and whoever controls the status quo is the standing beneficiary of that gap. The benign counter-position is that forking is arguably the intended ultimate mechanism of a permissionless system, in which case the gap is a design choice rather than a defect. (medium)

The trajectory is a stable exit and an advancing set of routes around it. The Knots migration has plateaued near a quarter of the network, and the power inversion it produced is stable rather than deepening. The implementation alternatives are the moving part, with Floresta improving and btcd and libbitcoin holding, while end-user captivity persists unchanged. The near-term horizon is the BIP-110 activation window around August 2026, the next occasion on which a contested question will test whether the system has any path other than exit. Through that window and the twelve to eighteen months around it, it is likely that contested consensus questions continue to resolve through exit and fork rather than through voice, because no binding arena has formed to absorb them and none is being built. Node operators will keep the credible exit they constructed, while end users and holders will remain loyal only in the sense that they have no exit to take, their loyalty a label for captivity rather than assent. (low-medium)

Remediation points at the gap named by the missing-arena finding rather than at any adoptable fix. The missing piece is a binding low-cost arena for contested policy, and within Bitcoin Core's governance form that mechanism may be structurally unavailable rather than merely unbuilt. Core is an open-source project with no membership rolls, no treasury, no charter, and a deliberately permissionless design. A body empowered to bind the losing side of a consensus dispute would concentrate the authority the design refuses, and forkability is arguably the intended substitute for it. The affordable moves are therefore narrower - better-structured deliberation in the venues that already exist, namely the mailing list, CoreDev, and Delving Bitcoin, which can reduce reliance on exit for routine disputes but cannot bind the contested ones. No binding mechanism exists that the project could adopt within its current budget, governance form, and size. No available remedy exists to recommend, and that absence is the outcome. (medium)

## 12. Borrowed Ground: Core runs on discretionary infrastructure and pseudonymous contributors a state kill-switch has already reached elsewhere.

Bitcoin Core depends entirely on discretionary third-party infrastructure - code hosting, a reproducible-build farm, continuous integration, and a developer mailing list - that each provider can withdraw at will, a dependency that has already failed once and that the state apparatus now prosecuting comparable software developers is positioned to weaponize into a kill switch (high). The dependency is not a defect of Core's engineering but the ground the project stands on: it owns its source code and its release-signing keys, yet it does not own, cannot compel, and holds no contract with any of the platforms through which that code is collaborated on, built, and distributed. Three findings compose the dynamic - a gatekeeper dependency on infrastructure that can be denied, the platform-specific risk concentrated in GitHub, and a latent government kill switch - and because they bear on a single shared target, the analytically decisive question is whether they are correlated.

**The mechanism** is an essential-facilities dependency (Areeda 1990): the infrastructure Core relies on is controlled by third parties who set terms unilaterally and can restrict or remove access without Core's consent. GitHub sits at the center as both the sole day-to-day collaboration surface and the point at which the other two findings converge - it is the specific facility the gatekeeper dependency rests on, the platform whose terms create platform risk, and the most likely conduit through which state pressure would reach a project the state has not targeted directly. The single-maintainer concentration analyzed in One Person Away From a Crisis is the human-capital face of the same single-point-of-failure family. Where that dossier treats the person, this one treats the infrastructure the person operates on.

**The gatekeeper dependency** is the load-bearing finding. Core relies on a stack of infrastructure, any layer of which can be discretionarily denied: GitHub for hosting (the same platform that deplatformed Tornado Cash contributors), the GNU Guix build farm for reproducible builds, Cirrus CI for continuous integration, and mailing-list hosting for developer coordination. The benign reading - that using best-in-class shared infrastructure is ordinary practice and that mirrors and forkability supply fallbacks - is the correct default across most of the stack, but it has already been falsified at one layer: the bitcoin-dev mailing list lost its Linux Foundation hosting and its archive went dark, a realized failure showing the mitigations were incomplete where they mattered. That single realized loss is what lifts this finding above the others. The dependency is not hypothetical.

**The government kill switch** (Vernon 1971) is the weaponization vector. The domain carries live state exposure: the Samourai and Tornado Cash prosecutions of software developers, the Tulip Trading fiduciary theory, GitHub's OFAC-driven takedown of the Tornado Cash repository, and the UK-ordered geo-block of the Bitcoin whitepaper on bitcoin.org. The same theory would reach Core's own contributors, some of whom operate pseudonymously. Core itself has not been directly targeted, and the 2025 Blanche memo together with the Van Loon decision narrowed the developer-liability theory, so the benign reading treats the kill-switch risk as latent rather than realized. That reading holds for Core specifically, but the mechanism has already fired on directly comparable targets - cryptocurrency software developers - and on GitHub hosting itself, leaving the Section 1960 developer-liability precedents on the books, which makes it a demonstrated and partly realized mechanism rather than a speculative one (medium-high).

**Platform risk** (Rochet and Tirole 2003) is the contested, GitHub-specific slice of the dependency. Day-to-day collaboration - pull requests, review, issues, continuous integration - runs inside GitHub, a Microsoft-owned platform that sets terms and could restrict access, require accounts, or remove the project, a risk the project's own developer wiki and issue tracker flag. The finding is contested, and the evidence favors the benign reading on the point that matters most: because merges are PGP-signed and mirrored and builds are deterministic, code integrity is independent of GitHub, so platform loss is a recoverable inconvenience rather than an existential event. What survives the challenge is the narrower residual - the accumulated audience and years of issue and pull-request history live on the platform and would not migrate with the code (medium-high).

**Who benefits and who pays** follows directly from the structure. The arrangement benefits Core in the ordinary course: discretionary infrastructure is free or cheap, best-in-class, and maintained by others, which is why a volunteer-scale project uses it. The cost is contingent and asymmetric - zero until a provider exercises its discretion, at which point it falls entirely on Core and its users, who have no contract to invoke and no recourse beyond the fallbacks. The providers capture the routine value. Core carries the tail risk.

The internal power structure is a dependency chain with a broker at its hinge (medium-high). Core depends on GitHub structurally and without substitute for collaboration, while GitHub is itself exposed to state pressure, making it the involuntary transmission point through which US DOJ / FinCEN / OFAC and allied regulators could reach the project. Against that exposure sits an organized counterforce: a developer-liability defense coalition - the Bitcoin Legal Defense Fund, and in the wider precedent fight the DeFi Education Fund, Coin Center, and the Bitcoin Policy Institute - occupies the negative space of the kill-switch finding, contesting the precedents that would give the state its lever. That coalition formed partly in response to Craig Wright's litigation, now largely spent. The fault line inside the counterforce is funding concentration: the defense is paid for by a small set of interested funders, so the developers whose legal survival it underwrites owe that survival to boards drawn from a narrow orbit.

**GitHub (Microsoft)** is the sole code host and the single point of failure the project itself flags. Its salience is Dangerous, though it currently reads Dormant - latent power at low urgency. Its power base is not native but derivative: coercive power it holds only as the involuntary delivery mechanism for state action, as when it suspended non-sanctioned Tornado Cash contributors tracking an OFAC designation. Core has consciously hedged below that apparent power with PGP-signed commits, mirrors, deterministic builds, and a live "Migrating Off GitHub" plan, which is why the exposure is low-probability, high-impact, and partially mitigated rather than acute. Its trajectory is that dependence deepened with the 2026 CI migration even as the mitigations matured.

**US DOJ / FinCEN / OFAC and allied regulators** are Dangerous, with contested legitimacy, and currently de-escalating. They reach Core not by targeting it but by setting liability precedents - Samourai, Tornado Cash and Storm - that define developer exposure, and by transmitting pressure through intermediaries, chiefly GitHub. The 2025 Blanche memo and the Van Loon decision softened the near-term posture, but the developer-liability precedents remain on the books and the stance is administration-dependent and can re-escalate (medium-high).

**Craig Wright / Tulip Trading** are Dangerous historically but largely neutralized as of 2026. The coercive litigation was entirely derivative of a fabricated Satoshi identity. Once that identity was exposed as forged - a fraud finding "on a grand scale," a worldwide anti-suit injunction, and contempt - the coercive base collapsed. Tulip Trading was the plaintiff proxy vehicle, backed by the BSV faction, and residual risk of further vexatious proxy filings is constrained by contempt exposure running through December 2026 (medium).

**Bitcoin Legal Defense Fund** is Dominant in salience, its urgency receded after the COPA outcome in 2024. It funds and coordinates a legal defense individual developers cannot self-finance - it took over the Tulip Trading defense and funded the 2023 defense of eleven developers - but it is one of Jack Dorsey's intermediaries, with a board drawn wholly from the Block and Chaincode orbit (Dorsey, Morcos, White). Its behavior is protective. The finding is the dependency and concentration it creates, since the developers whose legal survival it funds owe that survival to a board that decides whom to defend (medium-high).

**Coinbase (Coinbase Custody / Prime)** is a named dark stakeholder at Dominant salience shading toward Definitive. It custodies roughly 84 percent of US spot-ETF bitcoin - nine of twelve funds, on the order of $74-77 billion - a systemic single point of failure whose compromise or coercion would strike almost the entire ETF complex at once, and it is also an upstream funder of Core development through a $3.6M GiveCrypto grant to Brink. That custody dominance is structural leverage over Core's institutional-legitimacy base, not demonstrated capture, with Mt. Gox as the historical analog at far lower concentration (medium).

**Cirrus Labs / Cirrus CI (Cirrus Runners)** is a named dark stakeholder, Dangerous but decaying. It was Core's hosted-CI dependency and is shutting down on June 1, 2026 as Cirrus joins OpenAI - a realized infrastructure withdrawal, the second instance of the discretionary-infrastructure pattern after the mailing-list loss, that briefly made main-repo CI hostage to an external commercial roadmap. The fork fallback to free GitHub runners caps the blast radius, and the change was documented in the open rather than concealed (medium).

**Bitcoin Policy Institute** is a named dark stakeholder, Dominant and trending Definitive in its own legal and policy arena but peripheral to Core. It is the de facto legal and political shield for open-source Bitcoin development, shaping precedent - the Samourai defense, a July 2026 dormant-wallet intervention - that governs the environment Core contributors operate in. Its latent flags are funder concentration (Strive, Xapo, HRF, Vanguard Charitable), board overlap (Odell, Bailey), and a self-disclosed "protect our own bitcoin" motive that reveals partial rather than general representation (medium).

**Anchorage Digital** is a named dark stakeholder at contested low-to-medium salience: it is IBIT's added second custodian and thus the concrete occupant of the custody-diversification hedge against the Coinbase choke point, which makes it largely a mitigant rather than a risk (low-medium).

**Coin Center (Peter Van Valkenburgh)** is a named dark stakeholder at contested low-to-medium salience, a research and litigation shop defending the right to write and run code. It explicitly disclaims representing users, so its holder-representation leg withdraws, and it carries flagged board ties to Chaincode (Morcos) and a Brink funder (Pfeffer) (low-medium).

**DeFi Education Fund (Amanda Tuminelli)** is a named dark stakeholder at contested low salience, a litigation actor in the Roman Storm developer-liability case whose precedent bears on Core contributors. It is tangential to Core specifically, operating in the DeFi and Ethereum domain, and survives only as a defense actor in the precedent fight (low).

**GNU Guix project / official Guix build farm** is a named dark stakeholder at contested low-to-medium salience - Core's reproducible-build toolchain and default substitute server - but reproducible builds verified by many independent signers strongly mitigate loss of the official farm (low-medium).

**Independent Guix attestation signers** are a positional dark stakeholder, identified by role rather than name: the distributed set of non-maintainer builders whose guix.sigs signatures make release binaries trustworthy. The role is load-bearing - it is the mitigant to the build-farm single point of failure - and its value is structural, since withdrawal or compromise of the collective would break the release-trust model (low-medium).

**The trajectory is tightening.** Two of the four infrastructure layers have now failed or are failing on schedule - the mailing-list archive went dark and the CI provider is exiting - and the 2026 CI migration deepened rather than diluted GitHub dependence, even as code-integrity mitigations matured. The state vector is stable and currently de-escalated. Horizon: through 2028. The decisive uncertainty is correlation - whether a single triggering event closes several chokepoints at once rather than one at a time. Because the code host, the CI providers, and much of the distribution path sit in US jurisdiction under a shared OFAC and DOJ compliance driver, and because GitHub and its likeliest CI substitutes share exposure to the same designation mechanism, a single state action reaching more than one chokepoint simultaneously is a live structural path rather than a coincidence. Absent re-escalation from the current posture, a correlated multi-layer denial is unlikely over this horizon. Conditional on any return to the 2022-2024 enforcement stance, it becomes likely. Confidence in the correlation structure is medium. Confidence in its timing is low.

**The remediation path** is mostly already built. No specific concern was routed here, and the honest assessment is that Core has already adopted most of the remediation available to it: mirrors, forkability, PGP-signed merges, deterministic builds verified by independent signers, and a live migration plan together neutralize the code-integrity half of the risk within the project's existing budget and volunteer governance. Forkability, the stated fallback against infrastructure denial, is the same exit mechanism that Fork or Nothing identifies as dissent's only channel - here it is disaster recovery rather than governance. What has no mechanism is the other half: the correlation risk across chokepoints, and the portability of accumulated collaboration history and audience, which no current fallback addresses. That gap is structural rather than budgetary (medium).

## 13. Other Findings

Two surviving findings joined no compound dynamic.

- **Stakeholder pool.** The health of Bitcoin Core's contributor base is contested, and the most recent data cut against the stagnation reading (Putnam 2000): one sixteen-year study reports the pool is "not growing," but a Core maintainer counters on the project tracker that the retention metric "seems mostly stable," and 2025 recorded roughly 135 contributors against about 112 the year before, with mailing-list traffic up about 60 percent, per The Block. (low)
- **Demographic concentration.** Concentration appears on two axes (Rao and Argote 2006): reachable nodes cluster in the United States, at roughly 32 percent, and Germany, at roughly 17 percent - close to half the network in two countries, per btcnodes.io - while merge capacity concentrates in a handful of maintainers employed by a few funders, a concentration treated more fully elsewhere in this assessment. (low-medium)
